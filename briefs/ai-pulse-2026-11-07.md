# AI Pulse Brief

**Week of November 1–7, 2026**

The week's through-line is that the constraints on AI moved from engineering to politics and to the bill. Anthropic told prospective investors in its IPO filing that AI-driven acceleration may be imminent. Voters across the country then rejected local data centers at the ballot box. The cost of running AI agents surfaced in Elon Musk's admission that his personal agent (an AI assistant that works on a user's behalf) costs too much to serve. A new benchmark from SemiAnalysis, a chip and datacenter research firm, put the best model at solving 12% of expert-level work, which frames how much of the acceleration talk is hype.

---

## Top developments

### 1. Anthropic's amended IPO filing says its models may "imminently" cross its own threshold for AI-driven acceleration, and the week's other evidence makes the claim harder to dismiss and harder to prove

**What happened.** On November 2, Anthropic filed an amended registration statement (an S-1/A, the revised document a company files with the Securities and Exchange Commission before selling shares to the public). The new risk-factor language says internal estimates suggest the company "may imminently meet" the threshold in its Responsible Scaling Policy (Anthropic's published rules tying safeguards to model capability) for "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI" [[TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/02/anthropic-warns-that-its-very-close-to-rsi-capability-aka-self-building-ai/)]. The AI Daily Brief reports that the threshold means a year of progress equal to two years at the 2018–2024 pace [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03)]. This is recursive self-improvement, or RSI: AI systems doing enough of the work of building the next AI that progress compounds.

Anthropic also says that no current model has been designated as exceeding the threshold and that it is still assessing the data. The filing admits the company has "in the past not identified" significant acceleration until after it happened, and that it has already used AI to build models whose capability leaps it did not recognize at the time [[TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/02/anthropic-warns-that-its-very-close-to-rsi-capability-aka-self-building-ai/)]. The AI Daily Brief quotes a limit the company sets on itself: it does "not anticipate being able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models," adding "without meaningful human supervision or understanding" [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03)]. I found no copy of the filing text itself, so every quotation here comes from reporting on it.

Two other pieces of evidence arrived the same week. Sundar Pichai, CEO of Alphabet, said on the company's earnings call that he expects "continued acceleration because of RSI" over the next 12 months, per the AI Daily Brief's quotation [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03)]. I did not locate the call transcript, so that quote rests on the show alone. And researchers at MIT's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory published a result in which GPT-6 Astra, OpenAI's flagship model, edited the weights of a small open model [[VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/like-gene-editing-for-neural-networks-mit-researchers-used-gpt-6-astra-to-edit-qwens-open-weights-directly-boosting-math-performance)]. Weights are the numbers a model learns during training, and they are what makes a model behave as it does.

**Does the MIT weight-editing result hold up?** The result is real but narrow. Astra changed 131,072 of the roughly 800 million weights in Qwen3.5-0.8B, a small model from the Chinese company Alibaba, about 0.016% of them. The model's score on MATH-500 (500 competition-style math problems) rose from 7.9% to 48.1%, and its score on IFEval (a test of following written instructions) rose from 21% to 62.3% [[Four Week MBA](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-mit-openais-gpt-6-astra-edits-qwen-weights-math-500-up-6x/)].

The authors say the gain came from removing a looping failure in which the model repeated itself, not from adding math knowledge. On a different small model, Google's Gemma 3 270M, Astra could not improve math at all. The findings come from a research blog post and released logs, not a peer-reviewed paper [[Four Week MBA](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-mit-openais-gpt-6-astra-edits-qwen-weights-math-500-up-6x/)]. A senior Google researcher replicated the effect on a model with 20,486 parameters (adjustable numbers inside the model), against more than a trillion in frontier models [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03)].

**Both sides.** The dispute is whether a risk factor in a securities filing is evidence about the technology or a pitch to buyers.

- **It is marketing, at least in part.** TechCrunch's Julie Bort, a reporter at the technology news site, notes that an S-1 serves as both a disclosure and a sales document. On her reading, a claim that the company is close to self-building AI may be promotional [[TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/02/anthropic-warns-that-its-very-close-to-rsi-capability-aka-self-building-ai/)]. The filing gives no model name, date or underlying estimates, which leaves nothing for an outsider to check.
- **It should be taken seriously.** Bort also points out that Claude's abilities in coding, math and cybersecurity have grown sharply this year. A company that sells subscriptions to its own models gains little from saying they are dangerous, and securities law attaches liability to misstatements in a registration statement. That is the writer's inference, not a claim from the source.

**Why it matters.** The chain starts with what RSI would do to pricing and release schedules. If models do more of the research that produces the next model, the gap between releases shrinks. Buyers then face faster turnover of the tools they build on, and every vendor commitment signed today runs against a market that reprices more often.

The second step is financial. Anthropic's commitments for computing capacity run for years and cannot be cancelled, so the cost is fixed whatever demand does. A faster-improving product is the argument that revenue will grow into those commitments. Putting the argument in the filing means the company is telling investors the justification for the spending is the acceleration itself.

The third step is control. Anthropic's own sentence, that it cannot safely let Claude build successors without human supervision, sets the limit it says it will hold. The test is whether any lab publishes the estimates behind the threshold. Until one does, this is a disclosure no outsider can audit, and for diligence purposes it should be read as a stated risk with no measurement attached.

**My take.** The direction is signal and the label is hype. Three independent sources now point at AI contributing to AI research: a lab, a CEO and a university team. But the MIT demonstration is a small-model bug fix, and a 20,486-parameter replication says little about trillion-parameter systems. The unverifiable part is the "imminent" claim, and it should be weighed as a risk disclosure, not a measurement.

### 2. Voters rejected local data centers almost everywhere they were asked, and the stock market split into winners and losers that tells which AI businesses depend on new construction

**What happened.** On November 3, voters in ten states decided 41 local ballot measures on data center development, and Venable (a law firm that advises on real estate and technology deals) describes the results as "almost unanimously" against development [[Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2026/11/voters-approve-data-center-restrictions)]. 

The counts differ by who tallies them. AI Weekly reports that anti-data-center positions won 38 of more than 40 measures [[AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/ai-data-centers-lose-38-of-40-plus-us-ballot-measures)]. The AI Daily Brief says 43 of 44 moratorium-type votes passed [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 5](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-05)]. The sources count measures, jurisdictions and vote types differently, so no total here is settled.

The measures took several forms. Wells, Maine passed a retroactive 180-day moratorium on large facilities, and Ottawa, Kansas capped data centers at 25 megawatts. Several Ohio towns banned them outright, and Janesville, Wisconsin now requires a public vote on projects over $450 million [[Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2026/11/voters-approve-data-center-restrictions)].

The political results rest on the AI Daily Brief alone, which I could not verify independently. It reports that Democrats won the House and Senate, that Gina Hinojosa defeated Texas Governor Greg Abbott, and that Sherrod Brown won an Ohio Senate seat in a race fought largely over data centers [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 4](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-04)]. 

It also relays a Pew Research Center poll (Pew is a nonpartisan polling organization) finding that 61% of voters dislike candidates who back local data centers [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 4](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-04)].

The market reaction on Wednesday, again per that show, was split. The S&P 500 rose 1.6% and the seven largest technology companies gained $1 trillion, with Microsoft up 6.7% and Amazon up 6.1%. Nvidia (the dominant maker of AI chips) fell 3%, and CoreWeave fell 10%. CoreWeave rents access to GPUs (graphics processors that train and run AI models) to AI labs, and funds its buildings largely with debt [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 5](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-05)].

The same edition reports that Google, Meta, Amazon and Microsoft all came in under their third-quarter capital-spending targets because of construction delays. Microsoft attributed a $5 billion miss to a rented facility that was not ready on time. Oracle (which sells cloud computing and database software, and is building capacity for OpenAI) warned of possible force majeure on a power-starved New Mexico project. Force majeure is a contract clause excusing delay from events outside a party's control [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 5](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-05)].

**Both sides.** The dispute is whether a slower build-out hurts the companies that own AI infrastructure or helps them.

- **The constraint becomes a moat (a lasting competitive advantage).** Citrini Research, an independent investment research firm, wrote that "perhaps the bottlenecks become the excuse some people need to quietly trim their own projections without causing a rush for the exits" [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 5](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-05)]. On this reading, harder building means flatter chip prices, rising rents on existing capacity and lower spending. Margins and free cash flow (cash left after operating costs and investment) then expand, and the bubble deflates instead of popping.
- **The moratoria spook lenders.** Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW), host of the AI Daily Brief, a daily AI news show, lays out the disaster case: moratoria gain momentum and new governors move quickly. Wall Street pulls funding, neoclouds (companies like CoreWeave that rent AI computing power) fail, and a broader downturn follows [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 4](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-04)]. NLW calls it possible, not likely, which he says is why developers will work to find an accommodation.

**Why it matters.** The market split shows which businesses are exposed. Companies that already own capacity benefit when new supply is scarce, because the rent on what exists goes up. Companies that must build new sites with borrowed money are hurt, because every month of delay adds interest cost before any revenue arrives.

For the hyperscalers (Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta, the largest cloud providers), the delay has a second use. They were already behind their spending targets, and a political reason to slow down lets them cut next year's forecasts without conceding that demand is weaker than they said. Whether that is happening is checkable: if their fourth-quarter guidance lowers capital spending and cites permitting, the "exit ramp" reading gains support. If guidance rises anyway, the delays were a cost, not a cover.

For deal lawyers and operators, Venable's reading is the practical one. Charter amendments (changes to a town's founding document) are harder to reverse than ordinances. Retroactive moratoria mean a project already underway may not be protected. 

Perry, Ohio now requires developers seeking tax incentives to post financial guarantees. Site diligence, option agreements and financing conditions all need to track local ballot outcomes, and Venable expects state legislatures to propose many changes in 2027 [[Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2026/11/voters-approve-data-center-restrictions)]. For an operator in Florida, the AI Daily Brief notes it is the only one of the ten largest states with a Republican governor [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 4](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-04)].

**My take.** The split trading is the real signal, and the "exit ramp" story is a trading thesis, not a finding. It fits the numbers but it also fits a simpler story in which the market sold the leveraged builders and bought the cash-rich owners for the reasons above. The capital-spending guidance in the next earnings round will distinguish the two. The vote count is another place for caution: three sources, three totals, and no single official tally.

Source: [Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2026/11/voters-approve-data-center-restrictions) (measure types, deal implications); [AI Weekly on the ballot count](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/ai-data-centers-lose-38-of-40-plus-us-ballot-measures) (alternative tally); [AI Daily Brief, Nov 4](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-04) (election results, Pew poll, NLW's disaster scenario); [AI Daily Brief, Nov 5](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-05) (market moves, capital-spending misses, Citrini quote).

### 3. A new benchmark of expert-level work says the best AI model completes 12% of tasks, and Google's cheaper Gemini 4 Carbon comes close at a third of the cost

**What happened.** On November 2, SemiAnalysis published GDPx, a benchmark of agentic ability (the ability to carry out multi-step work with tools, unsupervised) across 11 domains the authors consider most valuable. The domains are chip design, cybersecurity, biology, finance, legal, quant trading, recursive self-improvement, hardware design, materials science, alignment (whether a model keeps to its intended goals) and software engineering [[SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/gdpx-measuring-frontier-model-capabilities)].

Each task runs in a realistic environment with an eight-hour limit. The headline score is the share of tasks passed on the first try, averaged over three attempts, with all domains weighted equally. Models run inside their makers' own agent software where it exists (Claude Code for Anthropic, Codex for OpenAI) [[SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/gdpx-measuring-frontier-model-capabilities)].

The authors state that "even the best model scores just 12%." Secondary summaries of the leaderboard give these scores. Claude Opus 5.5 is first at 12.0% and an average $51.41 per task. Gemini 4 Carbon is second at 10.9% and $16.29, and GPT-6 Astra third at 9.6% and $53.99. Meta's Muse Wonder Full is fourth at 9.0%, with no public price [[Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/gemini-4-carbon-trails-claude-opus-55-on-gdpx-at-lower-cost-636ee71f)]. My own arithmetic from those figures: Carbon reaches about 91% of Opus's score at about 32% of its cost.

**Does the 12% ranking hold up?** The ranking depends on which domains count. Safety classifiers (filters that block answers they judge dangerous) sometimes refused requests in the cybersecurity, biology and alignment tasks , and the authors say this "sandbagged" the overall scores of Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra. With those three domains removed, the OpenAI and Anthropic models lead, and Carbon is the only other model in the same range [[SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/gdpx-measuring-frontier-model-capabilities)]. A secondary summary says Astra moves to first in that cut [[Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/gemini-4-carbon-trails-claude-opus-55-on-gdpx-at-lower-cost-636ee71f)].

The dollar figures are also not what they seem. They are the average cost of running each task, failed runs included, so they are not the price of a successful result. The score is a single run by one evaluator on a new benchmark, with a reported 206 tasks that I could only confirm through the summary. And Carbon's access is limited to cyber defenders and partners, so most buyers cannot use it yet.

**Why it matters.** The most useful finding for this brief's readers is in the finance and legal domains. SemiAnalysis reports that those tasks are so hard that pass-or-fail scoring gives essentially zero for every model, so it switched to partial-credit averages for them [[SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/gdpx-measuring-frontier-model-capabilities)]. Professional work in those fields, done end to end, remains largely beyond current agents, even as vendors market them as replacing it. That supports a gap between what models can do on isolated questions and what a firm can hand them.

The cost column matters for a second reason. Suppose a model scores 91% as well and costs a third as much. Buyers who can access it gain leverage in price negotiations. The lab charging the premium, here Anthropic, then has to justify it with the remaining performance gap. 

The domain list also connects to item 1. SemiAnalysis reports that GPT-6 Astra leads the recursive self-improvement and materials science domains [[SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/gdpx-measuring-frontier-model-capabilities)]. The lab that warns most loudly about RSI does not lead the benchmark that tests it, which is a reason to treat the warning as a statement about Anthropic's internal estimates and not an industry-wide measurement.

**My take.** The 12% is the figure worth remembering, and the ranking is not. A benchmark where the leader fails 88% of expert tasks is a corrective to the week's acceleration talk in item 1, and it deserves more weight than any single model's position on it. I have not moved any row in the model standings on GDPx because it is one new evaluator whose cost figures I read only in summaries.

Source: [SemiAnalysis, GDPx](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/gdpx-measuring-frontier-model-capabilities) (domains, scoring method, caveats, finance and legal result; read directly, though the last part of the page was not); [Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/gemini-4-carbon-trails-claude-opus-55-on-gdpx-at-lower-cost-636ee71f) (scores and per-task costs).

### 4. Musk said his personal agent costs too much to serve, and Apple capped employees' AI spending the same week, which shows the cost of agents reaching the people paying for them

**What happened.** On November 1, Elon Musk replied on X that the cost of serving GrokBot, the personal AI agent from his company xAI, was unsustainably high relative to revenue. The post followed complaints that users had hit usage limits they had never reached before [[Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/musk-blames-unsustainable-costs-for-grok-bot-usage-limits-31dc833f)]. GrokBot works on tasks in the background, from research to browsing, which uses far more computing power than a chat reply.

Musk gave no figures for cost, revenue or the old and new limits, and I found no xAI document describing the change [[Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/musk-blames-unsustainable-costs-for-grok-bot-usage-limits-31dc833f)]. The AI Daily Brief notes that weeks earlier Musk had called GrokBot the fastest-growing product he had seen [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03)].

A second data point came from Apple. Mark Gurman, a Bloomberg reporter who covers Apple, reported that the company cut engineers' daily AI spending cap from $300 to $200, with a further cut to $100 possible. Non-engineers could drop from $100 to $25 a day [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 5](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-05)]. Apple's earnings call mentioned AI 45 times, a record, per the same show. Apple cannot be both pushing AI and rationing it without a cost problem underneath.

**Does the "unsustainable" claim hold up?** Only one fact is established: Musk said it, in one post. There are no numbers on cost or revenue. Much of the coverage comes from aggregator sites, and the AI Daily Brief's account of the reaction (that rival agents such as OpenAI's Dots and Anthropic's Duet gained users) is observers' reading, not data.

**Both sides.** The dispute is whether rising costs end the era of cheap AI or only sort its users.

- **The subsidy era is ending.** As NLW summarizes the bear case, venture money runs out, prices rise, users leave and valuations (the prices investors put on companies) fall [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03)]. The mechanism is that flat monthly plans were priced below the cost of heavy agent use, and a user running agents all day consumes far more than the plan pays for.
- **Users sort into two groups, not off a cliff.** NLW argues that some people find a cheaper model good enough and stay on $20–30 plans, while others get enough value to pay metered prices as a business cost [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03)]. Chamath Palihapitiya, a venture investor and founder of Social Capital, takes the middle ground, which is that too many power users are still subsidized by venture funding.

**Why it matters.** The chain starts with pricing structure. Under a flat subscription, the vendor bears the risk that a user consumes a lot. Under metered pricing, the user bears it. Moving from the first to the second shifts risk to buyers, which is why Apple is now capping per-person spend.

The second step is competitive. If cost per task, not accuracy, becomes the buying metric, models that are cheaper to run gain share. That is the logic behind this week's Carbon result in item 3 and behind the value-per-dollar rows in the standings below. A vendor that raises limits without notice also takes on a reputational cost: NLW notes users' anger was about the unannounced change, not the limit itself [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03)].

For an operator building on these models, the exposure is on both sides of the ledger. A product that resells agent work at a flat price carries Musk's problem. A buyer who signs a flat-fee contract should check whether the vendor can change limits unilaterally and on what notice.

**My take.** Musk's post is an anecdote, not a market signal, but Apple's cuts make it a pattern. I read both as the end of treating agent compute as free, and as a sign that the pricing question will matter more to buyers than any benchmark in the coming quarter.

Source: [Let's Data Science on the Musk post](https://letsdatascience.com/news/musk-blames-unsustainable-costs-for-grok-bot-usage-limits-31dc833f) (the statement and what is unknown); [Four Week MBA](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-musk-says-grok-bots-serving-cost-was-too-high-for-revenue/) (corroborating report); [AI Daily Brief, Nov 3](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-03) (bear and bull framing, Palihapitiya); [AI Daily Brief, Nov 5](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-05) (Apple spending caps, from Gurman's report, which I did not read directly).

## What people are saying

### a16z Podcast: Bending Spoons' CEO says its edge is debt discipline and patient buying, and that the software sell-off was mostly overvaluation, not AI

*Luca Ferrari is co-founder and CEO of Bending Spoons, an Italian company that buys software and internet businesses and runs them on a shared platform. Its purchases include Vimeo, Eventbrite, WeTransfer, AOL and, most recently, Airtable. He was interviewed by Anish Acharya, a general partner at Andreessen Horowitz who invests in consumer AI products, on the a16z Podcast, the firm's long-form show on technology and venture.*

Ferrari describes the model as a loop. Bending Spoons buys a software company, folds it into its platform, raises its earnings, and then borrows against the higher earnings to buy a larger one. He gives the arithmetic. Turn a business with 100 of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) into 200, borrow three times that, and buy more. The result compounds toward 100 times the starting value over a long period [[a16z Podcast](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/a16z-podcast/how-bending-spoons-built-a-20-billion-compounder-luca-ferrari)].

The part worth the operator's attention is the debt rules. He says borrowing is smart only while the risk of default stays negligible, and he notes that companies that went bust usually believed they were safe when they borrowed. His limits: leverage well below 3 times earnings (currently about 2.4 to 2.5), the ability to repay all debt before maturity even under severely adverse scenarios, annual refinancing to keep maturities five or more years out, and hedging all of the debt [[a16z Podcast](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/a16z-podcast/how-bending-spoons-built-a-20-billion-compounder-luca-ferrari)].

His second argument concerns software valuations. He says software-as-a-service stocks were "absurdly high" before the AI scare because they assumed 12% organic growth would last 15 years, and that AI gave investors an excuse to reset assumptions that never made sense. The reset valuations were broadly reasonable.

He does not accept that cheap code destroys these companies, because writing software has long been cheap relative to the value of winning a market. What protects them, he says, is first-mover advantage, habit and switching costs (the effort or risk that keeps customers from leaving). Network effects matter too, since Eventbrite's 90 million monthly ticket seekers cannot be copied. So does proprietary data, such as Komoot's history of trail conditions [[a16z Podcast](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/a16z-podcast/how-bending-spoons-built-a-20-billion-compounder-luca-ferrari)].

This sits against the week's reading on AI and software. Item 3's finding that the best agents complete 12% of expert tasks supports Ferrari's view that the replacement of established software is slower than the sell-off implied. His leverage discipline is the opposite of the debt-funded build-out in item 2. It may also explain why Berkshire Hathaway, the Warren Buffett-founded investment conglomerate, put another [$330 million](https://dealroom.co/news/162592-berkshire-hathaway-pours-another-330m-into-bending-spoons) into Bending Spoons in October: a buyer that borrows conservatively survives the cycle that strains the leveraged builders.

### The AI Daily Brief: Nathaniel Whittemore says AI will not make minds useless, because demand for intelligence has no known ceiling

*Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW), host and writer of The AI Daily Brief, a daily AI news and analysis show, discussing the essay "Cognitive Mercantilism" by Venkatesh Rao, which I read only through NLW's summary.*

Rao's frame is that fear of AI mirrors the old mercantilist belief that wealth is finite and that most people are good for nothing but farm labor. Capitalism disproved it when farming productivity freed most people from the fields and new wants created new work. NLW adds that the job-loss argument assumes people already demand as much as they ever will, which he calls "completely ahistorical" [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 1](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-01)].

His own contribution is a list of six ways AI could release pent-up demand: price, access, complexity, continuity (help that is always available), personalization and a human-trusted relationship. Each describes something people wanted and could not afford or reach. He also argues that agents "broke the idea of being done": at work, the backlog (the pile of work waiting) was always infinite and "done" was a negotiation shaped by human limits. With tireless copies of yourself, the whole backlog becomes reachable, and the only limit is imagining what belongs on it [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 1](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-01)].

NLW is explicit that this is not a case against safety concerns, and that "transitions between orders are never without cost." The argument runs against item 4's cost pressure, which suggests limits on agent use come from price, not from running out of things to do. It also reframes item 2 in the same terms: if demand for compute-hungry intelligence is unbounded, the fight over who pays for the power plants is the main constraint left.
