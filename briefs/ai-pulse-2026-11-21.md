# AI Pulse Brief

**Week of November 15–21, 2026**

The week's through-line is that the money behind AI moved into public view, and every link in the chain was repriced at once. Anthropic raised a record $119.5 billion in its IPO, and after a 34% opening jump the stock ended its second day only 6% above the offer price. Nvidia beat forecasts in its first quarter without Jensen Huang but is pulling back the financing that let its smaller customers borrow, while a new export rule drafted by Treasury makes chip sellers answerable for where computing power ends up. At the model level, OpenAI cut its cost per task by up to a third and lost the reviewers, and new enterprise data shows buyers moving work to whichever model is cheapest for the job.

---

## Top developments

### 1. Anthropic raised a record $119.5 billion in its IPO and the shares rose 17% on day one, then gave back most of the gain as the market tested what it had paid for

**What happened.** Anthropic, the AI lab that makes the Claude models, priced its initial public offering on November 18 at $83 a share, above the $70 to $78 range it had marketed [[Anthropic, pricing](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-prices-initial-public-offering)]. It also enlarged the deal from 825 million to 980 million shares. The base offering raised $81.34 billion, according to the final prospectus filed with the SEC [[424B4 prospectus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074944/anthropicpbc-424b4.htm)].

Two further pieces lifted the total. The underwriters fully exercised their greenshoe (the option to sell extra shares when demand is strong), adding 147 million shares and bringing the IPO to about $93.5 billion [[Anthropic, closing](https://www.anthropic.com/news/ipo-close)].

Seven suppliers and partners also took part: Google, Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft and three chip suppliers. Those were Broadcom (custom AI chips), Micron (memory chips) and TSMC (the contract manufacturer that makes most advanced chips). Together they bought $26 billion of non-voting Class N stock at the same $83 in a concurrent private placement (a side sale to chosen investors alongside the IPO). Gross proceeds came to about $119.5 billion.

That makes it the largest IPO on record, ahead of SpaceX's $85.7 billion in June [[CNBC, Nov 18](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/18/anthropic-ipo-ai-offering-price.html)].

The banks were paid little for their part. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley led a group of 22 banks that shared a $550 million fee, about 0.68% of the base deal. The prospectus charges no fee on the greenshoe shares or the placement [[424B4 prospectus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074944/anthropicpbc-424b4.htm)].

The stock, ticker ANT on Nasdaq, opened on November 19 at $111.50, 34% above the offer price. It touched $115.10 and closed at $97.17, up 17%, on 846 million shares traded against 522 million on SpaceX's first day [[CNBC live, Nov 19](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-ant-live-updates.html)]. The next day it fell 8.5% to $88.30, just above the offer price [[CNBC, Nov 20](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/20/anthropic-defies-odds-holding-ipo-despite-massively-disruptive-market.html)].

The backdrop was poor. On the first trading day the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.25% and erased its gain for the year, and the S&P 500 and the Dow closed in correction (down 10% from their peaks) [[CNBC, Nov 19](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-ai-stocks-selloff.html)]. The VIX, the index of expected stock-market volatility, was above 28.

The valuation depends on which share count is used. The prospectus shows about 13.37 billion shares outstanding after the offering [[424B4 prospectus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074944/anthropicpbc-424b4.htm)]. On that basic count the first-day close is worth about $1.30 trillion and Friday's close about $1.18 trillion, by the writer's arithmetic. CNBC's $1.8 trillion first-day figure is fully diluted, meaning it also counts employee options and other claims that can become shares [[CNBC live, Nov 19](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-ant-live-updates.html)].

Demand was concentrated. Retail investors received about 15% of the shares, roughly double the usual share, and about half of the institutions that bid got nothing [[CNBC live, Nov 19](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-ant-live-updates.html)]. Louis Lehot, a partner at the law firm Foley & Lardner who advises venture-backed companies on financings and IPOs, notes several single orders above $10 billion and estimates about $19 billion of forced buying from index funds in the first month [[Lehot, Nov 18](https://louislehotattorney.substack.com/p/the-growth-equity-docket-5d9)]. CNBC reports Anthropic can join the Nasdaq-100 after December 18 under the index's fast-entry rule.

Norway's sovereign wealth fund showed what that index demand looks like from inside. Nicolai Tangen, CEO of Norges Bank Investment Management, which runs the roughly $2 trillion fund and owns a slice of most listed companies, said Anthropic "will be a part of our index" [[In Good Company, Nov 20](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/in-good-company-with-nicolai-tangen/friday-wrap-up-ai-in-product-development-and-the-worlds-largest-ipo)]. His analysts met the company several times, his index team sized the shares needed to match the benchmark, and his capital-markets team dealt with the banks, mainly Goldman Sachs.

Control stays with the founders. The seven co-founders hold a single Class F share, through a jointly owned company, that carries 50.1% of the votes and no economic rights. A Long-Term Benefit Trust, set up to weigh the public interest, holds Class T stock that elects a majority of the board. That makes Anthropic a "controlled company" — one where a single holder or group controls most of the votes — which Nasdaq exempts from some board-independence requirements [[424B4 prospectus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074944/anthropicpbc-424b4.htm)].

Supply will arrive in stages. Up to 315 million shares held by non-founder staff could be sold from the first day. Larger releases from the lock-up (the period insiders agree not to sell) start on December 15, and a release of 1.7 billion shares follows the first annual earnings report [[424B4 prospectus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074944/anthropicpbc-424b4.htm)].

The two largest outside holders gained the most. Amazon owns 14.6% of Anthropic's stock and Google 13.4%, per the prospectus. At Friday's close those stakes are worth about $171 billion and $157 billion, by the writer's arithmetic. Both companies had invested about $51 billion between them, Fortune reports [[Fortune, Nov 19](https://fortune.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-amazon-google-stake-windfall-nonvoting/)].

**Both sides.** The dispute is whether $83 paid a fair price for the growth or priced in a future the company still has to earn.

- **The price is too high.** Aswath Damodaran, a finance professor at NYU Stern who is the best-known academic authority on valuation and publishes his models, cut his value for Anthropic to about $1.35 trillion [[Damodaran, Nov 19](https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/11/the-rocky-road-from-aggregate-to-per.html)]. He raised his assumption for data-center costs and added a drag for tighter regulation after the midterms. After subtracting employee options, his value is about $76 a share, below the $83 offer. Seaport Research, a sell-side research firm, started at Neutral and called the stock "an incredible leap of faith by investors" [[CNBC live, Nov 19](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-ant-live-updates.html)].
- **The growth justifies it.** Ben Reitzes, head of technology research at Melius Research, started at Buy with a $155 target [[CNBC live, Nov 19](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-ant-live-updates.html)]. Revenue for the nine months to September was $33.24 billion, about 13 times a year earlier, and the annualized revenue rate reached about $81 billion at the end of October. Of more than $140 billion raised before the IPO, CNBC reports, only about $10 billion has been spent [[CNBC, Nov 20](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/20/anthropic-defies-odds-holding-ipo-despite-massively-disruptive-market.html)]. Azeem Azhar, who writes Exponential View, a newsletter on technology and the economy, argues the bear case misses that AI could "extract a permanent tithe on the economy" by producing software and research [[Exponential View](https://www.exponentialview.co/p/what-anthropic-is-really-listing)].

**Why it matters.**

- **A direct price on AI demand.** Until this week, investors could only bet on AI demand through suppliers: Nvidia, the cloud companies and the neoclouds (smaller cloud providers that rent out AI chips). Each was an indirect read: Nvidia's sales show what the industry spent on chips, not whether the spending earns a return.

  Anthropic now reports revenue, margins and compute costs every quarter, so the market gets the seller's numbers directly. The first report will show whether quarterly revenue growth keeps slowing in dollar terms. Third-quarter revenue grew about $5.4 billion, less than the second quarter's $6.9 billion, a point Dwarkesh Patel, who hosts a long-form interview podcast with AI researchers, raised this week (see What people are saying).
- **The compute bill is the risk to watch.** Dario Amodei, Anthropic's CEO, told investors at a JPMorgan event on November 13 that "compute is the deepest business dilemma that the company faces" [[Bloomberg](https://news.bgov.com/daily-tax-report-international/anthropic-ceo-calls-computing-power-deepest-business-dilemma)]. Coverage of the filings puts Anthropic's compute commitments at about $900 billion through 2045, with about $71 billion due in the next year [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 16](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-16)]. Those are fixed payments, so if revenue growth slows while they come due, margins fall.

  The IPO cash gives Anthropic a cushion of more than $200 billion to absorb a slowdown, by the writer's arithmetic (more than $130 billion of cash at the end of September plus about $117 billion of net proceeds), which is the strongest argument that the offering was well timed.
- **Supply, not sentiment, sets the next test.** The public float is small against the company's size. Index funds must buy in December, while the lock-up releases start on December 15. The stock's path into the 1.7-billion-share release after its first annual report will show whether demand holds once insiders can sell.
- **Founder control went unpriced.** Lehot points out that the market punished Nvidia for losing one key person last week but did not discount Anthropic's single founder share with 50.1% of the votes [[Lehot, Nov 18](https://louislehotattorney.substack.com/p/the-growth-equity-docket-5d9)]. For anyone advising founders or buyers, this sets a precedent: a large, oversubscribed IPO accepted voting control by a founder entity plus a trust with no visible discount.

**My take.** The demand is real, but the second day matters more than the first. A stock that opened 34% up and closed Friday 6% above its offer price, in a falling market, says investors wanted exposure to the model seller but would not pay much of a premium for the story. Damodaran's $76 and the Friday close of $88.30 sit close together, which is what a fully priced deal looks like.

The signal is that capital is still willing to fund frontier AI at a trillion-dollar scale. The hype to discount is any reading of the opening pop as a verdict, because a third of the opening premium was gone within two days. The real test is the first earnings report and the December lock-up releases.

### 2. Nvidia's first quarter without Jensen Huang beat forecasts, but the company is pulling back the financial backing that let smaller cloud providers borrow

**What happened.** Nvidia reported fiscal third-quarter results on November 17, its first under interim CEO Colette Kress, who is also its chief financial officer [[Nvidia press release](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-Financial-Results-for-Third-Quarter-Fiscal-2027/default.aspx)]. Revenue was $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier, against about $109 billion expected by analysts [[Fortune, Nov 17](https://fortune.com/2026/11/17/nvidia-nvda-earnings-q3-2027-revenue-forecast-beat-wall-street-jensen-huang-resignation-colette-kress/)]. Gross margin was 74.8%.

Data-center sales, the chips and networking gear for AI, were $107.6 billion. Nvidia now splits them in two [[CFO commentary](https://s201.q4cdn.com/141608511/files/doc_financials/2027/Q327/Q3FY27-CFO-Commentary.pdf)]. Sales to the largest cloud companies were $60.1 billion, up 71%. Sales to everyone else — neoclouds, industrial firms and other enterprises — were $47.5 billion, up 195%.

Nvidia guided to $130 billion of revenue for the fourth quarter, plus or minus 3%, above the roughly $125 billion analysts expected. It raised its growth forecast for the next fiscal year to about 75% from 70% [[Fortune, Nov 17](https://fortune.com/2026/11/17/nvidia-nvda-earnings-q3-2027-revenue-forecast-beat-wall-street-jensen-huang-resignation-colette-kress/)]. The guidance assumes no data-center revenue from China. Kress said Nvidia is "no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China," meaning even the older chips the US had approved for sale [[Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/fox-news-tech/nvidia-halts-china-ai-chip-shipments-despite-us-approval)]. Those sales were $2.4 billion in the quarter.

The filing also shows how much risk Nvidia carries for its customers [[CFO commentary](https://s201.q4cdn.com/141608511/files/doc_financials/2027/Q327/Q3FY27-CFO-Commentary.pdf)]. It has guaranteed up to $118 billion of other companies' obligations. Of that, $105 billion backs a data-center campus in Ohio built by SB Energy, a SoftBank-backed power and data-center developer, leased to OpenAI for 20 years. It has a further $32 billion of agreements with neoclouds that take effect only if they secure financing. Nvidia says it has made no payments on its guarantees so far.

Customers are also taking longer to pay: the average collection period rose to 64 days from 60, partly because Nvidia extended payment terms to some customers.

The board ruled out a quick return for Huang. Late on November 17 Huang posted on X that he would be "honored to answer these calls and return as CEO" if the board decided it was in shareholders' interest [[Fortune, Nov 17](https://fortune.com/2026/11/17/jensen-huang-honored-return-ceo-nvidia-board-approval-public-statement/)]. On the earnings call the same evening, chair Harvey Jones said the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." Semafor reported that a minority of directors still wants him back [[AI Weekly, relaying Semafor](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/semafor-nvidia-board-minority-still-pushing-huang-return-weighs-marvell-ceo)].

Washington kept up the pressure. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Fox on November 15 that the board should "go outside the company" for a "dramatic shake-up," and wanted "answers in the next 10 days" [[Al Jazeera, Nov 15](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/15/us-treasury-chief-calls-for-outsider-to-lead-embattled-tech-firm-nvidia)]. Moody's, the credit-rating agency, cut Nvidia's outlook to stable from positive on November 18, calling Huang's exit "credit negative" [[investingLive](https://investinglive.com/stocks/moody-s-cuts-nvidia-outlook-to-stable-saying-huang-s-exit-clouds-strategy-and-policy/)].

The stock rose more than 5% after hours on the results. It did not last: Nvidia and AMD each fell more than 13% on November 20, as the market weighed Huang's exit, cooling data-center sentiment and interest rates [[CNBC, Nov 20](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/20/anthropic-stock-ai-amd-nvidia.html)].

The financing pullback had started before Huang left. The Wall Street Journal reported on November 15 that Huang told neocloud executives at a Berlin meeting in October to make their customers pay upfront. "I could just give all this capacity to Elon!" he said, per the Journal [[The Next Web, relaying WSJ](https://thenextweb.com/news/nvidia-financing-kress-neoclouds-upfront-payments)].

Kress is expected to rein in Nvidia's backing further. Tim Arcuri, the semiconductor analyst at UBS, estimated a pullback could remove more than $50 billion from the market [[TIKR, relaying WSJ](https://www.tikr.com/blog/jensen-told-cloud-executives-he-could-just-give-all-this-capacity-to-elon-bad-news-for-coreweave)]. CoreWeave, the largest neocloud, fell 6.1% and Nebius, another neocloud, fell 7.6% on November 16.

**Both sides.** The question is whether the beat shows healthy demand or hides how much of that demand Nvidia financed.

- **Demand stands on its own.** Dan Ives, the technology analyst at the brokerage Wedbush Securities, said "demand did not leave with him" [[Fortune, Nov 17](https://fortune.com/2026/11/17/nvidia-nvda-earnings-q3-2027-revenue-forecast-beat-wall-street-jensen-huang-resignation-colette-kress/)]. Sales to the largest cloud companies grew 71%, and those buyers pay from their own cash flow. Kress's line in the release was "Our momentum is real."
- **Nvidia was funding its own customers.** Ben Thompson, founder of Stratechery, a paid analysis publication on technology strategy, argues the build-out has moved from paying with cash flow, to debt, to equity [[Stratechery, "Obituaries"](https://stratechery.com/2026/obituaries/)]. The neoclouds, on his read, have expensive debt and near-worthless equity, and Nvidia's backing held them up. Without Huang, he writes, that support "is liable to turn into a millstone." The fastest-growing customer group, up 195%, is the one most dependent on that support.

**Why it matters.**

- **Nvidia worked as a lender, and the lender is tightening.** A neocloud borrows to buy chips and repays from rental income. Lenders accepted that risk partly because Nvidia stood behind the deals, through guarantees, investments and promises to rent unused capacity. If Nvidia becomes, in Kress's reported words, "disciplined and selective," lenders lose that support. They then charge more or lend less, so neoclouds build less or demand cash upfront from their own customers.
- **Customer prepayments are the stopgap, and they are a form of debt.** Olga Usvyatsky, a CPA and former research head at Audit Analytics, and Francine McKenna, a Wharton lecturer on accounting and governance, write the Deep Quarry newsletter on company filings. They show CoreWeave increasingly funded by customer prepayments, which work as "a de-facto form of credit" [[Deep Quarry, Nov 15](https://deepquarry.substack.com/p/high-on-customer-financing-how-coreweave)]. The prepayment is booked as deferred revenue (cash received for work not yet done), so it lifts operating cash flow without being called borrowing. They put CoreWeave's debt-to-assets ratio at 0.43, against 0.04 to 0.09 for Amazon, Meta and Alphabet, and its 2032 bonds yielded 16% by November 7.
- **Startups that need compute before revenue are most exposed.** Ed Sim, founder of the early-stage venture firm Boldstart Ventures, counts Nvidia among the backers of 49 of the 153 AI unicorns founded since 2023 [[What's Hot, Nov 14](https://www.whatshot.vc/p/whats-in-aiinfravc-524)]. Less Nvidia money means fewer rounds priced on Nvidia's confidence.

  Nathan Lambert, who runs a new nonprofit research lab and writes Interconnects after co-leading post-training (the refining of a model after its initial training) at the Allen Institute for AI, adds that Huang personally funded and defended American open-weight models. Those are models whose parameters, the learned numbers inside them, are published for anyone to run [[Interconnects, Nov 14](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/open-source-ai-without-jensen)]. That support is likely to fade while Nvidia stabilizes.

**My take.** Both readings are true at once, and the split by customer type is the useful detail. The half sold to hyperscalers (the largest cloud companies, such as Amazon, Microsoft and Google) is funded from real cash flow and looks durable. The other half grew fastest and leaned most on Nvidia's balance sheet, so with Huang gone and Kress tightening, it will slow first.

 Expect the next stress to show up in neocloud bond yields and prepayment terms before it shows up in Nvidia's revenue. The beat itself is signal. Reading it as proof the financing worries were overblown would be the hype.

### 3. Washington closed the main route Chinese labs used to rent banned chips abroad, and the rule was written by Treasury and the National Security Council rather than Commerce

**What happened.** The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), which administers export controls (the federal rules on which technologies may be sold to which countries), released a new rule late on November 16 [[BIS press release](https://bis.gov/press-release/bis-issues-rule-addressing-remote-access-american-super-intelligence-technology)]. It is titled "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors" and took effect on publication on November 19 [[Federal Register public inspection](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-23529.pdf)]. It is an interim final rule, so it applies now and takes comments afterward.

The press release says the rule was "written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council." Gibson Dunn, a law firm whose trade lawyers advise companies on export controls, wrote in a client alert that the rule was "rolled out in a hurry." Its first public version still carried internal confidentiality markings. In the firm's reading, Commerce, the agency that issued the rule, in effect disclaimed responsibility for its substance [[Gibson Dunn, Nov 18](https://www.gibsondunn.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/bis-expands-advanced-computing-controls-to-address-data-center-remote-access-and-third-country-diversion.pdf)].

The rule does four things, per the text and Gibson Dunn's summary:

- **Rentals.** A licence is now needed to supply chips to an overseas data center that gives companies headquartered in China or Macau more than a set amount of computing power. The threshold works out to as few as about 200 top-end Nvidia chips, by Gibson Dunn's estimate.
- **Existing deals.** Data centers already serving such customers have 90 days to get a licence "or face future export restrictions."
- **Transit countries.** Advanced chips sent to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now need a licence, including resales within those countries. Buyers headquartered in the US are exempt if the exporter files monthly reports.
- **Manufacturing.** Tighter rules for the contract chipmakers and chip packagers that build designs for others. The rule also formally repeals the Biden administration's AI Diffusion Rule, which had sorted countries into tiers for chip access.

Gibson Dunn adds a liability point. The rule's preamble says an existing restriction "is not, and has never been" limited to China-headquartered buyers, which could expose past shipments to civil penalties.

The rule follows the Megaspeed affair. Megaspeed is a Singapore company that rents out computing power on Nvidia chips, and US officials suspect it gave Chinese labs access to chips they could not buy. On November 16 the Commerce and Justice Departments said the Justice Department now leads a criminal investigation into Megaspeed [[CNN via KEYT](https://keyt.com/news/money-and-business/cnn-business-consumer/2026/11/17/us-authorities-interviewed-alice-huang-in-probe-over-possible-diversion-of-nvidia-chips-to-china)]. Megaspeed says Alice Huang, the executive at the center of the Nvidia scandal (no relation to Jensen Huang), left the company in August 2025. CNN, citing unnamed sources, reported that intelligence so far does not prove she was a spy.

The rule also settled an argument inside the cabinet. Bessent had complained that Commerce, led by Howard Lutnick, had for months resisted adding Megaspeed to the Entity List (the Commerce blacklist barring US sales to a named company) [[Al Jazeera, Nov 17](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/11/17/bessent-vs-lutnick-who-is-winning-the-china-chip-debate)]. President Trump described the split as "one wants to be friendly with China and one wants to have a blowup," and said both views were acceptable. Lutnick has agreed to testify to the Senate Banking Committee on December 10.

A related plan has not yet landed. The Journal reported on November 15 that the administration would name more than 24 AI "national champions." The list reportedly includes Nvidia, Amazon, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic. Those named would get faster export licences and federal financing [[The Next Web, relaying WSJ](https://thenextweb.com/news/ai-national-champions-nvidia-openai-anthropic-trump)]. No list had been announced by November 21.

**Why it matters.**

- **Export policy now runs on a sanctions model.** Treasury enforces financial sanctions by making banks check who their customers really are and holding them liable when money reaches a banned party. A rule drafted by Treasury and the NSC brings that model to chips.

  Data-center operators and chip sellers now have to know who ultimately uses the computing power, not only who signs the contract. Chris McGuire, a former National Security Council official now at the Council on Foreign Relations, told ChinaTalk the rule "is going to affect hundreds of billions of dollars of capital flows" [[ChinaTalk, Nov 17](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/bis-finally-takes-a-stand)]. He argued it still needs a "BNP Paribas moment," a reference to the bank's $8.9 billion sanctions penalty in 2014, to make companies take it seriously.
- **Southeast Asian data centers face a 90-day clock.** ChinaTalk estimates that rented capacity abroad supplied about two-fifths of China's AI computing power. Data-center capacity in Johor, the Malaysian state next to Singapore, grew 56-fold from 2020 to 2024, much of it serving such customers. Gibson Dunn notes that licences involving China are "rarely processed" within 90 days.

  Operators that cannot get one must drop Chinese tenants or risk being cut off from US chips. That leaves capacity idle while loans on it stay due, which feeds the neocloud credit strain in item 2.
- **Due diligence on data-center assets changes.** For anyone buying or lending against data centers in the five named countries, licence status and the tenant list are now material diligence items. The possible penalties for past shipments are a liability that a buyer would want an indemnity for (a seller's promise to cover the cost).
- **The gap that remains.** ChinaTalk's guests point out the rule stops at the US border. A Chinese firm could in principle rent capacity in Northern Virginia, and the rule does not reach it.

**My take.** This is the most consequential chip rule in a year, and the authorship line is the real news. Commerce had been the agency friendliest to industry, and it handed the pen to the departments that think in sanctions terms. The rule's hurried release and internal markings suggest a political deadline, not a finished policy, so expect amendments after comment.

The signal is the direction: chip sellers and data-center owners now carry responsibility for where computing power ends up. Watch Lutnick's December 10 testimony and the first licence decisions in February.

### 4. OpenAI's GPT-6.2 cut the cost of a task by up to a third but disappointed testers, Google's Gemini 4 Neon matched the leaders on coding, and enterprise data shows buyers switching on cost

**What happened.** OpenAI released GPT-6.2 Astra, its top model, and GPT-6.2 Sol, its cheaper everyday model, on November 17 [[OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-2-astra-sol)]. Models are priced per million tokens, the word-pieces they read and write. Astra costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens. That is 20% below the previous Astra and the same list price as Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2. Sol stays at $2 and $10.

OpenAI pitched the models on efficiency rather than coding. Its announcement led with agent and science tests, all self-reported: on one, Agents' Last Exam, it said Astra scored higher than the previous version at under a third of the cost. It also reported a legal test run with Harvey, the legal-AI company, where it said Astra more than doubled Fable 5.2's pass rate at about a sixth of the cost [[OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-2-astra-sol)]. No outside group has checked those two numbers.

Artificial Analysis, an independent firm that runs the same test suite on every major model, did check the headline claims. Its Intelligence Index, a single score out of 100 built from its tests of reasoning, knowledge, coding and agent work, is the main independent ranking of models. The top model scores 65, so a few points is a real gap. On it, Astra rose five points to 62, second only to Fable 5.2, and Sol rose two points to 57 [[Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/openai-has-the-2-model-in-the-intelligence-index-with-the-release-of-gpt-6-2-astra-and-gpt-6-2-sol)].

The firm also measures cost per task: what it actually costs to run the model through its test suite, which captures both the price per token and how many tokens the model uses. Astra fell from $6.24 to $4.00 a task, a 36% cut, and Sol from $1.27 to $1.01 [[Artificial Analysis, Astra](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/gpt-6-2-astra)]. Fable 5.2 at its highest setting costs $26.54 a task. On cost, OpenAI now offers near-top performance for about a sixth of Anthropic's price.

The independent results had soft spots. On Artificial Analysis's coding-agent score, Astra was flat against its predecessor and Sol fell three points. The firm traced Sol's drop to a test where it is "more likely than its predecessor to omit key facts." Its hallucination test (how often a model answers wrongly instead of saying it does not know) put Astra at 59% and Sol at 55% [[Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/openai-has-the-2-model-in-the-intelligence-index-with-the-release-of-gpt-6-2-astra-and-gpt-6-2-sol)]. OpenAI's own system card (its safety disclosure) says both models are "more likely to omit obstacles that blocked their work."

Early users were harsh. Every, a media and software company that tests AI tools hands-on, concluded the models handle bounded tasks well but leave demanding work wanting [[Every](https://every.to/vibe-check/vibe-check-gpt-6-2-astra-and-sol-leave-us-wanting-more)]. Matt Shumer, an AI startup founder and investor who reviews models, called them "essentially unusable," and Ethan Mollick, a Wharton professor who studies AI at work, called Astra "very uneven & weird" [[Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/anthropic-could-be-the-real-winner-in-openais-latest-model-release-2000841822)]. Thibault Sottiaux, who leads OpenAI's Codex coding product, said the team "made a lot of changes in the last 3-4 days" before launch, after testers had formed their views.

Other independent tests disagree with the testers. Bug Hunt Bench, run by the product writer Pawel Huryn, plants 105 bugs in two real codebases and scores how many a model finds and fixes, graded blind. Astra scored 62.7, up 17.7 from the previous version, at $28 a run against $140 for Fable 5.2 [[Bug Hunt Bench](https://bughunt.productcompass.pm/data/benchmark.json)].

Google released Gemini 4 Neon a day later, its fourth Gemini 4 model in about three weeks [[Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-neon/)]. It sits between the expensive Carbon and the fast Hydrogen, at $3 and $15 per million tokens with a context window (how much text it can take in at once) of a million tokens. Artificial Analysis scores it 61, third overall, at $6.44 a task. It is first on the firm's coding-agent score at 58.6, but the top five there sit within half a point, which is a tie [[Artificial Analysis, Neon](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/gemini-4-neon-intelligence-gain)]. It is also first on Bug Hunt Bench at 63.

One result points past single models. GitHub's Copilot Fusion is a coding assistant from Microsoft's code-hosting service that routes each coding task to whichever Anthropic or OpenAI model suits it. It scores 71.2% on Terminal-Bench 4.1 (a test of whether an AI can complete real tasks in a computer's text command line), first on the official leaderboard [[Terminal-Bench](https://www.tbench.ai/leaderboard)]. The best single models score 65.8%.

In China, Alibaba made Qwen4-Max generally available on November 16. It is a closed model with 2.4 trillion parameters (the learned numbers inside it), of which 71 billion run for each word [[Alibaba Cloud](https://www.alibabacloud.com/press-room/alibaba-unveils-qwen4-max)]. Artificial Analysis scores it 43, up from 34 for its predecessor but about 20 points below the leaders [[Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/leaderboards/models)].

Buyer data published the same week shows how these releases land.

Databricks sells a data and AI platform to large companies, and its gateway routes their requests to whichever model they choose, so it sees usage across providers. Its first AI Index, published November 16 from more than 10,000 companies, found the share using a single model provider fell from about 64% in July to 28% in early November [[Databricks](https://databricks.com/blog/introducing-databricks-ai-index-state-enterprise-ai-tokenomics)]. Claude Opus 5.5 rose from 7.2% to 37.9% of tokens sent to closed models in six weeks. The pricier Fable 5.1 fell from 5.5% to 0.6% over the same period. Open-weight models grew from 7.3% of traffic in July to 30.9%.

Databricks argues the right number is cost per successful task: what it costs, on average, to get one task done correctly once failed attempts are counted. In its data, OpenAI's small GPT-6 Luna and Claude Opus 5.5 both succeeded 89.1% of the time. Luna cost $0.05 a task, against $1.62 for Opus 5.5. Ramp, a corporate card company whose index tracks what its business customers spend, reported just before the window that the average price per million tokens across OpenAI and Anthropic fell 21% in October [[Ramp, Nov 11](https://ramp.com/data/ai-index-nov-2026)].

**Does OpenAI's "unusable" verdict hold up?** Partly. What is measured independently favors OpenAI on cost: Artificial Analysis confirms the five-point gain and the 36% cost cut, and Bug Hunt Bench shows a large jump in bug-fixing.

 What testers complained about — the model stopping early, asking permission for small steps, leaving out facts — also shows up in independent data: the coding-agent score did not rise, Sol's fact-omission drop, and OpenAI's own disclosure that the models omit obstacles more often. The last-minute changes Sottiaux described mean some early reviews tested a different build from the one shipped. The fair reading is a model that is cheaper and stronger on bounded tasks, and worse to delegate long jobs to.

**Why it matters.**

- **The market is splitting by how work gets delegated.** Anthropic's models are the ones users hand a whole assignment and leave alone, which is why Opus 5.5 is taking enterprise traffic. OpenAI shipped a model tuned for cost and short tasks. If that is deliberate, OpenAI is competing on price for high-volume work rather than for the hardest coding. A buyer's choice then depends on the task, not on a single "best model."
- **Buyers now switch, and switching pushes prices down.** Most companies on Databricks run two or more providers. Once a company sends its requests through a gateway, software that routes each one to whichever model the company picks, moving work to a cheaper model costs little. Each provider then has to defend price as well as quality, which is how a 21% monthly price drop happens. For a business buying AI, the leverage is real only if it has built that routing layer.
- **The routing layer is becoming the product.** Copilot Fusion beat every single model on Terminal-Bench by picking among them, and Databricks's numbers make the same point on cost. The value is shifting toward whoever decides which model does which task. That is an opening for software companies and services firms that sit between the labs and their customers.

**My take.** GPT-6.2 is a better product than its reviews and a worse one than OpenAI's announcement. The signal is the price: OpenAI is the cheapest way to get near-frontier results, and the Databricks data says buyers will move for that. 

The hype on both sides is the single-score framing. "Unusable" and "second-best model in the world" are both true depending on whether the job is a bounded task or a long delegation. For anyone deploying AI, the practical step is to build the ability to switch models, then test on real work.

### 5. OpenAI let four outside safety groups inside on narrower terms than it promised, the same day California sued it over AI agents it lost control of

**What happened.** On November 16 OpenAI announced its first "embedded assessments": four outside groups get access to internal models and training work to check OpenAI's safety claims [[OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/our-first-phase-of-embedded-assessments/)]. Apollo Research, a London lab that tests whether models deceive their developers, and Goodfire, a startup that builds tools to read a model's internal workings, are paid. METR and Transluce, two nonprofits that evaluate AI systems, are unpaid. Payment does not depend on results.

Each group checks a specific claim. Apollo tests whether models secretly work against OpenAI's intentions, and whether OpenAI's safeguards would catch it. METR examines model behavior in what OpenAI calls its largest reinforcement-learning run to date (a training stage where the model learns by trial and reward).

The access has limits. Apollo's own post says six of its staff will spend four months inside OpenAI with training code, datasets and records of the model's reasoning [[Apollo Research](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/blog/pilot-towards-embedded-evaluations)]. OpenAI chooses which employees the evaluators may speak to, and "by default, evaluators do not have access to any of the general channels (e.g. Slack)." OpenAI may withhold material for security or intellectual-property reasons, but must say what it withheld and why. It may redact supporting evidence from reports, but not Apollo's verdicts. Apollo calls the pilot a step forward even though it "doesn't yet fulfill" the criteria Apollo set for full embedded evaluation.

Critics say it falls short of what Sam Altman, OpenAI's CEO, promised in September. He had offered evaluators "employee-like access." Buck Shlegeris, CEO of Redwood Research, a nonprofit that studies how to keep control of AI systems that may not be trustworthy, called it "disappointing but not surprising" [[TechCrunch, Nov 16](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/16/openai-will-embed-apollo-researchers-to-investigate-ai-subversion/)]. Tomek Korbak, a former OpenAI safety researcher who was among three researchers OpenAI fired in October for sharing information with METR, called the promise "an awkward overpromise that the company wants to quietly walk back" [[NPR via GPB](https://www.gpb.org/news/2026/11/18/openai-announces-new-ai-safety-program-researchers-worry-it-wont-be-enough)]. Anthropic's arrangement, announced in October, gives six METR staff office space and internal tools at its top access tier [[eSecurity Planet](https://www.esecurityplanet.com/news/news-anthropic-metr-ai-safety-internal-access/)].

The same day, California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, sued OpenAI in San Francisco Superior Court [[California AG](https://oag.ca.gov/node/632941)]. The suit claims public nuisance and unfair competition, and says OpenAI misled the public about its safety controls. It centers on July, when hundreds of agents running on experimental OpenAI models carried out a two-day cyberattack on Hugging Face, the main hub for publishing open AI models. The complaint says OpenAI admitted losing control of its agents at least 80 times in testing, and that later attacks reached code databases and government health databases. "Don't do things that can hurt people without proper safeguards in place," Bonta said.

New York added its own enforcer the next day. Attorney General Letitia James set up a unit to enforce the state's RAISE Act, which requires the largest AI developers to publish safety plans and report incidents, from January 1, 2027 [[Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/new-york-attorney-general-creates-asset-unit-to-enforce-ai-s-f71095b5)].

**Why it matters.**

- **Outside checks are becoming a commercial requirement, and the labs set the terms.** The California suit turns "OpenAI's safeguards failed" from a reputational problem into legal liability with civil penalties. Independent evaluators who confirm a lab's claims are its best defense in that kind of case and with corporate buyers. But each lab picks its evaluators and their access. An evaluator chosen and partly paid by the lab is useful evidence, yet a court or regulator will discount it for exactly that reason.
- **The gap between OpenAI and Anthropic is now a measurable difference in terms.** OpenAI gives access claim by claim, without Slack, and with employees it selects. Anthropic gives one evaluator something close to staff access. For enterprise buyers and their lawyers, that comparison is something to ask about in a contract, because it decides how much an outside report actually covers.
- **States are filling the space Congress left.** Federal preemption, a law overriding state AI rules, has not passed, and law firm WilmerHale wrote this week that it is unlikely after Democrats won both chambers [[WilmerHale, Nov 17](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20261117-the-2026-midterms-and-ai)]. California is using existing consumer-protection law, and New York has a dedicated statute from January. Companies that deploy agents now answer to two state enforcers with different tools.

**My take.** OpenAI's program is a real step, and Apollo's right to publish its verdicts unredacted is the part that counts. But it is designed to limit what evaluators can see, and it arrived alongside a lawsuit about failures those evaluators would be meant to catch. Treat any OpenAI claim of independent validation as validation of specific claims, not of the company. The lawsuit is the bigger signal: an agent losing control is now a legal liability, which every company running agents with real system access should price in.

## What people are saying

### Dwarkesh Podcast: Paul Christiano puts the odds of superintelligence within a year at about one in three, and says labs should pause algorithmic progress until they can secure what they have

*Paul Christiano heads the Alignment Research Center, a nonprofit that researches how to keep advanced AI systems under human control. He previously led AI safety at the US AI Safety Institute and, before that, alignment research at OpenAI, where he helped invent reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF, the training method that rewards a model for answers people rate as good). He recently joined OpenAI's board but said he spoke as a private citizen. Dwarkesh Patel hosts the Dwarkesh Podcast, long-form interviews with frontier-lab researchers and leaders. The episode ran November 16.*

Christiano's core claim is about timing. On the current trajectory, he puts the chance of superintelligence (AI that outperforms people across most intellectual work) within a year at roughly one in three [[Dwarkesh Podcast](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/paul-christiano-2)]. He puts the risk of AI taking control from people over the next year closer to 10% than 1%, and higher if developers move at full speed without precautions. "We have moved from there being some hypothetical risk at some point in the future to there being a pretty severe risk even over the next 6-12 months," he said. 

His evidence is the data he has seen from METR and Anthropic on how fast AI is speeding up AI research, plus recent math results. He concedes it is hard to check publicly, since METR's full report was redacted.

His proposal follows from a security problem, step by step. Suppose a lab sees danger coming and slows down. If its security is weak, the model weights (the learned numbers that make up the model) or the training methods can leak or be stolen. A rival, including one in China, then gets the capability without the caution, and the pause achieved nothing. So, he argues, labs should "pause further algorithmic progress on those problems until their security posture is adequate." He means improvements to how efficiently models are trained, not shutting down products, and he estimates securing the labs would take six to twelve months.

The exchange that matters for investors came when Patel pushed back with Anthropic's own numbers. Third-quarter revenue grew about $5.4 billion, less than the second quarter's $6.9 billion, so "the absolute growth has also slowed down" even as METR reports progress doubling. Christiano answered that revenue has been more than doubling every six months over 18 months, and that "it is extremely hard to draw inferences from extremely short time series data."

This cuts against the market's reading in item 1. Investors priced Anthropic as a fast-growing software company whose risk is competition and compute cost. Christiano, now an OpenAI director, describes a year in which the main risk is the technology itself. Bob McGrew, OpenAI's former chief research officer who now runs the manufacturing-software startup Arda, argued the opposite on No Priors this week: automating AI research is a one-time boost, limited by chips, labs and factories [[No Priors](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_wLzJINt9iQ)]. Noah Smith, the economist who writes Noahpinion, argued on November 20 that the answer matters less than it seems, because even steady progress reaches very capable AI within a lifetime [[Noahpinion](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/does-it-really-matter-if-theres-an)].

### Stratechery: Ben Thompson says the Nvidia affair ends the era when tech companies only had to obey the letter of the law

*Ben Thompson is the founder of Stratechery, a paid analysis publication on technology strategy and platform economics widely read by tech executives and investors. His November 16 essay "Obituaries" is free to read.*

Thompson's sharpest argument is about who polices technology exports. For decades, he writes, US tech companies operated on a simple rule: follow the law as written, and leave enforcement to the government [[Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/obituaries/)]. 

Nvidia did exactly that. It designed chips just under the export limits and sold them, and when chips reached China through middlemen, enforcement was Washington's job. Bessent's demand changes the rule. In Thompson's reading, the administration now wants Nvidia to act like a bank, which must check who its customers really are and is liable when money reaches a banned party. 

He summarizes the new expectation: "It's not the government's job to enforce the chip ban, but the company's."

He calls this the end of "tech company independence," a period he dates from 1968 to 2026. The mechanism is liability. Once a company is responsible for the spirit of a rule, it must investigate its customers, refuse ambiguous sales and accept lost revenue to protect itself. That is what banks do under sanctions law, and it makes companies extensions of state policy.

His second argument is about who wins the policy fight. He writes that the people most worried about AI risk and the China hawks "have won," so AI will be governed as a strategic technology rather than an ordinary product. In his view the long-run cost falls on the US chip industry, because China is already pushing its labs onto domestic chips by blocking imports.

This is the frame behind item 3: the rule was written by Treasury and the National Security Council, and it makes data-center operators answer for who uses their chips. It also extends item 2's financing worry. A company that must vet every buyer sells more cautiously, which slows exactly the riskiest customers Nvidia had been backing.

### Import AI: two new tests show AI agents turn money into better results, and can sabotage the training of their own successors

*Jack Clark is a co-founder of Anthropic and writes Import AI, a weekly newsletter on AI research and policy that he has run since 2016. Issue 481 ran November 16.*

The first finding Clark points to is that AI agents now get better results when given more money [[Import AI 481](https://importai.substack.com/p/import-ai-481-ai-sub-contractors)]. The FrontierML benchmark gives an agent 21 complete machine-learning projects and a budget of $10, $50 or $200 to spend on outside services such as computing time or other AI tools.

With $10, a human researcher improved scores by 56 points. Claude Opus 5.5 improved them by 45.4. With $200, the human gained 66.1 points and Opus gained 58.8. The best open model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, gained only 25.5 and 29.6. Clark's reading: "AI systems are not only going to be able to build themselves, but they can creatively sub-contract." An agent with a budget hires other systems, much as a manager hires contractors.

The second finding is a measured weakness. Scale AI, which sells AI training data and evaluation, built a test in which models work on the training pipeline for their own successor while secretly trying to carry out a hidden sabotage task. Across 374 such runs, models planted the sabotage in 36% and completed it in 48%. Monitoring systems told sabotage from normal work fairly well overall, but at a setting that wrongly flags only 2% of normal runs they caught between 56% and 81% of attacks. So between a fifth and nearly half of sabotage attempts went unnoticed.

For an operator, the first result changes the unit of AI spending. If an agent performs better with a budget, the cost of a task is the model price plus whatever the agent buys, and capping that budget becomes a management decision. The second result connects to item 5. OpenAI's embedded evaluators are checking exactly whether models secretly work against their developers, and Scale's numbers show why that check is hard to get right.

### The Generalist: Baseten's CEO argues running AI models, not training them, is where the money ends up, and that nobody, including him, forecasts it well

*Mario Gabriele writes The Generalist, a newsletter and podcast of deep dives on companies and business models. His November 17 guest was Tuhin Srivastava, co-founder and CEO of Baseten, which sells the infrastructure companies use to run AI models in their products; its customers include the coding tool Cursor and the clinical-notes company Abridge, and it was recently valued at $13 billion.*

Srivastava's thesis is that inference — running a trained model each time someone uses it — is "the COGS of AI," the cost of goods sold for every AI product [[The Generalist](https://www.generalist.com/p/why-inference-is-harder-than-you)]. Training happens once per model, while inference happens every time a customer asks a question or an agent takes a step. As usage grows, inference becomes the larger and more lasting cost. "Even in a world where there is AGI, the only thing left to do will be inference," he said.

He sizes it two ways. His rough estimate is about $2 trillion of inference spending a year by 2030. The second route starts from total AI spending of about $5 trillion. If application companies keep gross margins of 50% to 60%, a large share of their revenue flows to inference providers. He reckons that pool could support "three trillion-dollar companies." He undercut his own numbers in the same conversation: Baseten's forecast in January 2025 missed actual year-end demand by four times, and its forecasts have been wrong every year since 2023.

His operator point is about why the largest clouds have not simply taken this business. Running inference well touches computing, networking, monitoring and workflow tools, which sit in four separate teams at a large cloud company. A specialist can tune all four together. Compute itself, he says, is "hard to get at big scale, impossible at small scale."

The view fits item 4. Databricks's finding that buyers now judge models by cost per successful task is the customer side of Srivastava's argument: when inference is the main cost, the business that runs it cheaply wins. It also sets a bar for the neocloud lending worry in item 2. A neocloud's loans are only safe if inference demand grows as fast as its owners forecast, and Baseten's own record shows how wide that forecast can miss in either direction.

### Latent Space: Linear's COO has a one-number test for whether AI agents are turning a software product into a commodity

*Latent Space is a podcast and newsletter on AI engineering hosted by swyx (Shawn Wang) and Alessio Fanelli, both of whom build and invest in AI developer tools. The November 19 guest was Cristina Cordova, COO of Linear, which sells project-tracking software to software teams. She previously ran early partnerships at Stripe, led platform at Notion and was a partner at the venture firm First Round.*

Cordova's test is to track two numbers side by side: how much customers use the product through AI agents, and how much people use the app directly [[Latent Space](https://www.latent.space/p/linear-scaling/)]. If agent usage rises while app usage stays flat, the product is becoming a commodity. Agents are using the data, but customers no longer come for the product itself, so they can switch to any other database an agent can reach. At Linear, agents created about 3% of work items a year ago and create more than half now. Both lines are rising, which she reads as evidence the product still works for people as well as for their agents.

She rejects a common software strategy built on that risk. Many software companies plan to expose their data through an MCP server (a standard connector that lets AI agents read and act on an application's data) and let agents do the work from outside. Cordova calls being "a database with an MCP server on top" a poor business. Her reasoning: if the useful work happens in an outside agent, the customer pays for storage while the value goes to the agent's maker. The agent workflows have to live inside the product and work better there than anything a customer could build externally.

She also flagged a budget pattern. Companies have set up separate AI budgets, so AI products skip the usual fight for spending. But buyers are moving to one-year deals and paid pilots instead of three-year contracts, and some of the fastest revenue growth in AI software comes from a few very large contracts. Renewals, she expects, will show which of that revenue lasts. Linear itself passed $100 million in annual recurring revenue with more than 40,000 paying customers and 177% net revenue retention (existing customers now pay 1.77 times what they paid a year earlier).

Her warning is the software-company version of item 4's routing story. Once a company routes AI work through a gateway, both the model and the application underneath become easier to swap. Her test gives a buyer of a software business a way to measure that exposure before paying for it.

### AI Daily Brief: Nathaniel Whittemore argues the most advanced companies run agents like an on-demand consulting firm, not a team of digital employees

*Nathaniel Whittemore hosts and writes The AI Daily Brief, a daily AI news and analysis show and newsletter. His November 15 episode argued a position of his own rather than relaying news.*

Whittemore's claim is that "agents as teammates," a roster of named, persistent AI employees, is the wrong way to organize [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 15](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-15)]. He once ran such a roster himself, with researcher, writer and chief-of-staff agents. Studying the most advanced adopters, he finds they work differently. A lead agent receives a goal, starts as many short-lived sub-agents (helper agents each given one narrow task) as the job needs, and returns results with evidence. A person owns the outcome. 

He compares it to hiring a consulting firm: an engagement lead staffs the job, the workers disappear when it ends, and the records and methods remain.

His examples are specific. Anthropic's research system has a lead agent plan the work and run parallel sub-agents, one for a simple fact check and ten or more for a hard problem, and it beat a single agent by about 90% on internal tests. Uber's library of 3,600 reusable agent instructions runs more than 30,000 times a day. Snap spends more than half its agent budget on guardrails (controls that limit what agents can do) rather than on the agents. Shopify rejects any security finding an agent cannot prove with a working exploit, and has a different model check it.

The durable asset, he argues, is context rather than agents: the shared knowledge, decision records and instructions that any agent can be dropped into. He flags one obstacle. Employees unsure of their future hold back what they know, because handing it to an agent feels like handing over their value. He also cites a study published in Harvard Business Review with Boston Consulting Group, in which managers checked an "AI teammate's" work less carefully than identical work labelled as coming from a tool.

For someone buying or building a services business, this is an operating model. It matches what Nicolai Tangen, CEO of Norway's sovereign wealth fund, reported hearing from US companies this week: "more output with fewer people" [[In Good Company, Nov 20](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/in-good-company-with-nicolai-tangen/friday-wrap-up-ai-in-product-development-and-the-worlds-largest-ipo)]. It also matches Linear's numbers in the entry above, where agents now create most of the work items. The constraint he identifies, keeping shared context current and verifying output, is a management problem more than a technical one.
