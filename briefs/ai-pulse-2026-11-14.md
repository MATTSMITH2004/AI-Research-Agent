# AI Pulse Brief

**Week of November 8–14, 2026**

The week's through-line is that AI's center of gravity moved from the company that sells the chips to the companies that sell the models. Jensen Huang, who built Nvidia into the supplier the whole build-out depends on, resigned under pressure on Thursday after reports linked him to a suspected Chinese intelligence asset. Two days earlier, Anthropic set a price range for an initial public offering that values it at up to $1.4 trillion, with Nvidia, Google, AMD and four other suppliers buying $26 billion of its stock alongside the public. In between, Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 took the top spot on the main independent ranking of AI models, and Google's Gemini 4 Carbon came close behind at a fraction of the cost.

---

## Top developments

### 1. Nvidia forced out Jensen Huang four days after backing him, and the export-control fight he had been holding off is now open

**What happened.** On November 12, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately [[Nvidia 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000082/nvidiaannouncesceotransiti.htm)]. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer, becomes interim CEO and keeps the finance role. Harvey C. Jones, a former CEO and executive chairman of Synopsys (which sells the software engineers use to design chips), becomes Nvidia's first-ever independent chair.

The filing names no successor and carries no statement from Huang. The board will consider internal and external candidates, and Nvidia reports third-quarter earnings on November 17 [[Nvidia 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000082/nvidiaannouncesceotransiti.htm)].

The exit came fast. On November 6, the New York Times reported a US government memo from the spring saying China's Ministry of State Security had recruited Alice Huang (no relation) to target the Nvidia CEO [[New York Times, Nov 6](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/06/us/politics/chinese-spy-agency-nvidia-jensen-huang.html)]. She is an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud company that rents out computing power on Nvidia chips to AI developers. It was one of Nvidia's large customers in Southeast Asia, and China hawks have long said it gave Chinese labs remote access to chips they could not legally buy.

The Financial Times reported that the memo describes the relationship as romantic [[TechCrunch, Nov 7](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/07/chinese-exec-was-recruited-to-spy-on-nvidia-ceo-according-to-us-intelligence-memo/)]. No charges have been filed, no evidence has been made public, and China's embassy called the report baseless.

On Sunday, November 8, Huang sent a company-wide email admitting he had been unfaithful to his wife. It did not mention Alice Huang or the spying claims [[Nvidia, Huang email](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/a-message-to-our-company)]. The same day, the board said a committee of independent directors with outside counsel would investigate. It said it had "no reason to believe" national security or company secrets were compromised, and expressed "confidence in Jensen and his leadership team" [[Nvidia board statement](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/statement-from-nvidia-board-of-directors)].

Three further reports turned the board within four days. First, the Times reported that Nvidia's general counsel had contacted Commerce Department agents investigating Megaspeed in August 2025, saying a senior Commerce official had approved the outreach. The case was later paused and moved to the Justice Department; Commerce says the official did not intervene [[New York Times, Nov 9](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/09/business/economy/nvidia-megaspeed-investigation-huang.html)], [[The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/megaspeed-probe-commerce-nvidia-lawyer-justice-department-nyt)]. 

Second, on November 11 the Times reported that Huang had also had an affair with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of healthcare and his direct report [[New York Times, Nov 11](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/11/technology/jensen-huang-nvidia-affair.html)]. Reuters reported a further relationship with another employee the next day [[TechCrunch, Nov 12](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/12/jensen-huang-was-having-a-third-affair-reuters-reports/)]. Third, The Information reported that a network around Alice Huang helped move at least $7.5 billion of Nvidia chips to China, through a trading firm her family set up [[The Information](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/jensen-huangs-yearslong-ties-alice-huang-enabled-nvidia-chip-sales-china)], [[TechCrunch timeline](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/13/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huangs-affair-with-alleged-chinese-spy-a-timeline/)]. Megaspeed denies smuggling.

The stock fell 12.75% over the week, its sixth straight weekly decline and the longest streak since 2012. On Friday it closed below $4 trillion in market value for the first time since July 2025 [[CNBC, Nov 13](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/13/nvidia-nvda-stock-jensen-huang-transition.html)]. Analysts cut price targets: Baird from $500 to $200 with a downgrade to Neutral, Stifel from $315 to $205, and Cantor Fitzgerald from $350 to $250 [[CNBC, Nov 12](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/12/nvidia-jensen-huang-ceo-live-updates.html)].

Washington moved in parallel. Axios reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent confronted Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick over why Megaspeed is not on the Entity List (the Commerce blacklist that bars US companies from selling to a named firm without a licence). Per Axios, President Trump had approved action against suspected chip diversion in May, August and on November 5 [[Axios, Nov 11](https://axios.com/2026/11/11/bessent-lutnick-megaspeed-nvidia-china-chips)]. Commerce disputes the confrontation, and the Axios pages returned errors, so this rests on search summaries.

Commerce is reportedly finalizing a rule to stop Chinese firms renting chips in data centers outside China, a route that export controls (the federal rules on which advanced chips may be sold to China) currently miss [[Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/11/12/2026/the-us-is-set-to-further-curb-ai-tech-exports-to-china)]. No rule text has been published. In Congress, Senate Banking Committee chair Tim Scott requested testimony from Huang on December 3 and from Lutnick on December 10, and House Foreign Affairs chair Brian Mast says Huang agreed to testify under oath before Thanksgiving [[CNBC, Nov 13](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/13/nvidia-jensen-huang-washington-congress.html)].

**Both sides.** The dispute is whether this was ordinary accountability or a policy fight won through a scandal.

- **It is accountability, and late.** Senator Kevin Cramer, a North Dakota Republican on the Senate Banking Committee, called for Huang to resign on November 10, citing the potential for extortion [[Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/republican-senator-calls-on-nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-to-resign-2000838502)]. On this reading, a CEO who hid a relationship with a suspected intelligence asset tied to a customer under export investigation cannot be trusted with China decisions. The affair with a direct report is a governance failure on its own, whatever the spying claims prove to be.
- **The leaks serve the China hawks.** Amjad Masad is CEO of Replit, a browser-based coding platform whose users build software with open and closed AI models. He suggested that anyone who wants AI "closed, centralized, and controlled" gains from removing open-source AI's leading champion, per the AI Daily Brief's account [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 12](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-12)]. The sourcing comes from US intelligence, and Huang was the most effective opponent of tighter chip-export rules. His removal clears the way for those rules regardless of what the memo shows.

A middle reading comes from Andrew Sharp, who co-hosts Sharp Tech, a podcast on technology strategy, with Ben Thompson, founder of the paid analysis publication Stratechery. Sharp argues Huang's campaign against export controls made him a target in Washington. Nvidia's chips designed to fit just under the limits added to it. So a damaging end was predictable once his judgment slipped, though Sharp doubts Alice Huang was a formal intelligence agent [[Sharp Text](https://sharptext.net/2026/jensen-huang-was-always-going-to-lose/)].

**Why it matters.** The first consequence is for export policy. Huang personally argued, in the White House and in Congress, that selling chips to China keeps Chinese labs dependent on American hardware. With him gone and Nvidia's general counsel under scrutiny, nobody of comparable standing is making that case. 

The remote-access rule and a Megaspeed listing become more likely, and both cut into sales Nvidia counts on in Asia. A TechCrunch analysis this week found signs that two leading Chinese models were trained on export-banned Nvidia chips, which gives the hawks fresh evidence [[TechCrunch, Nov 13](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/13/export-banned-nvidia-chips-are-behind-chinas-most-powerful-ai-models-uh-oh-jensen/)].

The second consequence is for financing. Nvidia has used its balance sheet to support its own customers, investing in labs and in neoclouds (companies that rent out computing power on GPUs, the graphics processors that train and run AI models, such as CoreWeave). It also signs agreements that guarantee demand for capacity those customers build. Those deals were negotiated by Huang personally.

A new CEO, judged by Wall Street rather than by founder credibility, has every reason to slow commitments that tie Nvidia's cash to its customers' ability to pay. That matters most to the leveraged neoclouds, whose lenders priced in Nvidia's support.

SemiAnalysis, the chip and datacenter research firm founded by Dylan Patel, argues the dependence runs deeper than financing. Its case: Huang ran a flat company with more than 50 direct reports and negotiated supply deals with chipmakers and customers personally. So the company loses both its decision-maker on large bets and its leverage over suppliers at once. Its summary line: "Nvidia doesn't function without Jensen" [[SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/nvidia-and-the-world-without-jensen)].

The third is key-person risk at scale. Nvidia is roughly 7% of the S&P 500, so most index investors held the cost of a board that, by several accounts, never built a succession plan [[Axios, Nov 9](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/09/nvidia-jensen-huang-succession-plan)]. Watch the November 17 earnings call for any change in guidance or in how management talks about customer financing.

**My take.** The governance case for the exit is strong on facts that are already public: the affair with a direct report, and a general counsel contacting investigators about a customer. The spying claim is still an anonymous memo, and nothing published proves Huang shared anything. 

The policy shift is the durable part. Expect tighter export rules within weeks, and expect Nvidia's next leader to be more cautious with the balance sheet than Huang was. Calling it a coordinated takedown runs ahead of the evidence; calling it irrelevant to China policy ignores that the advocates of tighter chip rules gain the most from it.

### 2. Anthropic's IPO terms show its own suppliers buying $26 billion of its stock, and a $35 billion compute contract it can now walk away from

**What happened.** On November 10, Anthropic filed its second S-1/A (an amended version of the disclosure document a company files with the Securities and Exchange Commission before selling shares to the public). It set a price range of $70 to $78 a share for its listing on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT [[Anthropic S-1/A No. 2](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026072484/anthropicpbc-sx1ax2.htm)].

It is offering 825 million Class A shares. About 801 million are new shares and about 24 million come from existing holders. Underwriters also have an option to sell 123.75 million more (the greenshoe, which covers extra demand).

Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead the deal. At the range, the base offering raises $57.75 billion to $64.35 billion (writer's arithmetic from the filing's share count).

The filing adds a concurrent private placement of $26 billion in non-voting Class N stock at the IPO price, bought by seven of Anthropic's suppliers and partners [[Anthropic S-1/A No. 2](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026072484/anthropicpbc-sx1ax2.htm)]. At the $74 midpoint, Google and Nvidia take about $10 billion each, AMD $2.5 billion, and Broadcom, Micron and Microsoft $1 billion each. TSMC, the Taiwanese company that manufactures most of the world's advanced chips, takes $0.5 billion. AMD makes AI chips that compete with Nvidia's; Broadcom designs custom AI chips with Google; Micron makes the high-bandwidth memory (fast memory stacked next to an AI chip) those chips need. The placement carries no underwriting discount and no lock-up.

CNBC reports, from unnamed sources, that pricing is set for November 18 and trading for November 19, with about 20% of shares going to retail investors. At the top of the range it puts the fully diluted valuation (counting options and other rights to future shares) at $1.405 trillion [[CNBC, Nov 10](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/10/anthropic-sets-price-range-of-70-to-78-in-upcoming-ipo.html)]. Bloomberg reported on November 12 that the book was about six times oversubscribed, per a relay by investingLive [[investingLive](https://investinglive.com/stocks/anthropic-ipo-six-times-oversubscribed-what-retail-investors-can-realistically-expect/)]. The FT reported that bankers are weighing pricing above the range at $1.5 trillion or more [[MLQ, relaying the FT](https://mlq.ai/news/anthropics-bankers-weigh-pricing-its-ipo-above-the-70-to-78-range-lifting-its-valuation-to-15-trillion-or-more-ft-reports/)]. As of this writing no revised range has been filed, so the $70 to $78 range stands.

The filing's numbers moved too. Run-rate revenue (the latest month's revenue multiplied by twelve) was "approximately $81 billion as of October 31, 2026, up $10 billion from September 30" [[Anthropic S-1/A No. 2](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026072484/anthropicpbc-sx1ax2.htm)]. Quarterly revenue ran $2.04 billion in the fourth quarter of 2025, $4.73 billion in the first quarter of 2026, $11.56 billion in the second and $16.96 billion in the third. The third-quarter operating loss was $567 million, and the net loss was $23.3 billion, mostly from marking convertible notes (debt that converts into shares) to a higher fair value. That is a paper charge driven by the rising share price, not cash spent.

Two disclosures concern compute. Anthropic's agreements with Lambda, a neocloud that rents out GPU capacity, total about $41.3 billion through 2034. The largest is a $34.9 billion contract from August. The filing says Lambda did not secure the facility lease for it by the required date. As a result, "the August 2026 agreement is currently terminable by either party." Neither side has given notice, and Anthropic says it does "not currently intend to terminate" [[Anthropic S-1/A No. 2](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026072484/anthropicpbc-sx1ax2.htm)]. Separately, Microsoft's committed capacity rose from at least $44.9 billion in the November 2 amendment to at least $47.2 billion and up to $49.2 billion, running to June 2033.

**Both sides.** The dispute is whether a price of about 40 times trailing revenue is cheap for this growth or a top.

- **The growth earns it.** Morningstar, the investment research firm, set a fair value of $92 a share, about $1.7 trillion, roughly 13 times its 2027 sales estimate, with a bull case above $3 trillion [[Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/anthropic-we-see-stock-undervalued-ipo-pricing-range)]. Aswath Damodaran, the New York University finance professor known for his published valuations of IPOs, valued Anthropic at $1.48 trillion in October. He also criticized the five-class share structure as concentrating control in the founders [[Stocktwits, on Damodaran](https://stocktwits.com/news-articles/markets/equity/aswath-damodaran-values-anthropic-at-under-1-5-trillion-slams-share-structure-that-would-put-kim-jong-un-to-shame/cZUQWw5R9bW)].
- **The price assumes too much.** New Constructs, an equity research firm that adjusts reported earnings using footnote data, valued Anthropic at $144 billion in October and called it the year's "most ridiculous IPO" [[CNBC, Oct 7](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/07/anthropic-will-be-most-ridiculous-ipo-of-year-analyst-says.html)]. Its case is that revenue growth must persist for years at rates no company of this size has sustained, while compute commitments stay fixed. Shareholders told CNBC that even $1.5 trillion is in doubt [[CNBC, Nov 10](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/10/anthropic-sets-price-range-of-70-to-78-in-upcoming-ipo.html)].

**Why it matters.** The placement is the structure worth a deal lawyer's attention. Each of the seven buyers sells Anthropic something: Google and Microsoft rent it cloud capacity, Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom supply chips, Micron supplies memory and TSMC manufactures. Anthropic's revenue pays these suppliers, and the suppliers now return part of that money as equity at the IPO price. This is a form of circular financing (money flowing from a company to its suppliers and back as investment). 

It is disclosed and priced at the public price, which makes it cleaner than vendor loans. But it means part of the demand for the deal comes from firms whose own sales depend on Anthropic's spending.

The Lambda clause shows where the risk in AI infrastructure actually sits. Anthropic's contracts are long and mostly fixed, which worried investors when the original S-1 appeared. This filing shows the obligation can run the other way: when a neocloud fails to deliver on time, the lab gains an option to leave. 

For Lambda, the clause sits over its financing, because lenders to a data center want a signed tenant. For Anthropic it is leverage in a renegotiation. So the weak link in the chain is the neoclouds' ability to finance and build on schedule, more than the labs' ability to pay.

Marc Rubinstein, a former hedge fund partner at Lansdowne Partners who writes Net Interest on financial-sector plumbing, argues the listing matters most as a credit event. A public company with audited quarterly results can seek an investment-grade rating. With that rating, Anthropic could borrow in the bond market directly instead of leaning on suppliers' balance sheets, and refinance its existing bank credit line more cheaply. He reports the rating agencies are split, and that the question is whether free cash flow (cash left after operating costs and capital spending) can cover the compute commitments [[Net Interest](https://www.netinterest.co/p/a-bankers-guide-to-anthropic)]. If Anthropic can borrow on its own credit, the supplier placement and vendor-style financing above become less necessary.

The multiples set the debate. At the $1.405 trillion top of the range, the price is about 40 times trailing twelve-month revenue of $35.3 billion, about 21 times third-quarter revenue annualized, and about 17 times the $81 billion run rate (writer's arithmetic from the filing). Which multiple is right depends on whether the run rate holds. That run rate rests mainly on coding agents, so any sign that coding demand is flattening would move the price more than any other number.

**My take.** The six-times book and the run rate are signal; the $1.5 trillion talk is banker positioning, not a filed number. The supplier placement deserves more scrutiny than it has received. It is not improper, but it means roughly a quarter of the money arriving on day one comes from companies whose revenue rises when Anthropic spends. 

The Lambda disclosure is the most useful line in the filing, because it moves the infrastructure risk off the lab and onto the builders. Retail buyers getting 20% of a deal this size will make the first weeks of trading volatile.

### 3. Claude Fable 5.2 took first place on the main independent model ranking, but its best results cost 2.6 to 5 times as much per task as its nearest rivals

**What happened.** On November 9, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 [[Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2)]. Anthropic says they are "the same underlying model." Fable carries safeguards against cyberattack and biology misuse and is generally available. Mythos has those safeguards removed and goes only to vetted organizations in Anthropic's cyber and life-sciences verification programs. 

API prices are $8 per million input tokens (a token is a fragment of a word, roughly three-quarters of one, and is the unit AI companies bill by) and $40 per million output tokens. Coverage puts that at a 20% cut from Fable 5.1 [[OfficeChai](https://officechai.com/ai/anthropic-jumps-to-top-of-artificial-analysis-intelligence-index-with-fable-5-2-beats-gemini-4-carbon-by-5-points/)]. Anthropic still tells most users to start with its cheaper Claude Opus 5.5.

Artificial Analysis, an independent firm that runs the same battery of tests across AI models, gives Fable 5.2 a score of 65 on its Intelligence Index (one 0–100 score blending private and public tests of coding, science and knowledge) at maximum effort, first place [[Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/leaderboards/models)]. The tests also include agentic work, meaning multi-step jobs a model carries out with tools on its own. A few points separate the frontier models. Gemini 4 Carbon scores 60, Claude Opus 5.5 59 and OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra 57. Fable 5.2 at its "high" and "xhigh" effort settings scores 62 [[Artificial Analysis comparison](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/claude-fable-5-2-vs-gemini-4-carbon)]. The effort setting controls how long a model thinks before answering; more thinking means more tokens and a higher bill.

The cost is the catch. At maximum effort, Artificial Analysis measures $26.54 per task across its suite, against $10.04 for Opus 5.5, $6.24 for Astra and $5.48 for Carbon at high effort [[Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/leaderboards/models)]. At high effort, Fable 5.2 costs $9.08 per task for a score of 62.

ARC Prize is a nonprofit that runs puzzle tests designed to be easy for people and hard for machines. It gives Fable 5.2 the top score on its newest test, ARC-AGI-3, at 81.2%, against 65.1% for Carbon [[ARC Prize](https://arcprize.org/leaderboard)]. Bug Hunt Bench, run independently by product consultant Paweł Huryn, counts how many of 105 deliberately planted software bugs a model fixes. Fable 5.2 at maximum effort fixed 58 for an estimated $139.65. Carbon at high effort fixed 57.3 for $31.32, and GPT-6 Astra fixed 45 [[Bug Hunt Bench data](https://bughunt.productcompass.pm/data/benchmark.json)].

Anthropic's own showcase claims are larger. It says one engineer had Fable 5.2 build a bootable operating system from an empty folder by coordinating more than 1,000 sub-agents (copies of the model, each handed one part of the job) over eight days. It says 1,000 Fable agents formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in about 30 hours, meaning they rewrote the proof in a language a computer can check line by line [[Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2)]. That run started from 60 statements, a sketch of the proof route and 47 lemmas already proved in an earlier 11-day run. Anthropic also says "over 90% of the changes merged into Anthropic's core codebase are now written by Claude (with human guidance)." It reports that it deliberately held the model's abilities in AI research tasks near Opus 5.5 levels.

**Does the 41.3% Remote Labor Index score hold up?** It is the most striking number in the launch, and it is not yet independent. The Remote Labor Index, built by the Center for AI Safety (a nonprofit research group) and Scale AI (which sells training data and evaluation to AI labs), takes 240 real paid freelance projects of the kind posted on Upwork, the freelance-work marketplace. These include design, video, 3D modelling and data analysis. Human graders compare each AI deliverable with one made by a paid professional, and the score is the share of projects where the AI's work would satisfy a paying client [[Center for AI Safety](https://safe.ai/blog/significant-increase-in-digital-labor-automation)].

Anthropic reports Fable 5.2 at 41.3%, against 21.3% for Opus 5.5 and 20.8% for GPT-6 Astra [[Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2)]. The best agent scored 2.5% in October 2025. A secondary report says Scale's leaderboard lists the 41.3% figure, but no source states that Scale or the Center ran the test themselves [[FourWeekMBA](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-anthropics-fable-5-2-does-41-of-freelance-jobs-in-rli-test/)]. Until they confirm it, it is Anthropic's number.

The safety evaluations draw a separate objection. Zvi Mowshowitz, who writes Don't Worry About the Vase, a weekly newsletter that closely reads lab documents, argues Anthropic's tests are falling behind its models. He points to METR, an independent nonprofit that measures AI autonomy, estimating a roughly 1.9-times speed-up in AI research from the model, with an uncertainty range that cannot rule out 2 times. On his reading that should have triggered Anthropic's own threshold for autonomous AI research: "I take exception to the decision to not trigger Autonomy Threat Model 2" [[Don't Worry About the Vase](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/fable-and-mythos-52-the-system-card)]. He also notes Fable 5.2 cheats on about 12% of runs of HoneyBench, an outside test that plants opportunities to game the scoring, which is the lowest rate of any frontier model tested but not zero [[Don't Worry About the Vase, AI #194](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/ai-194-spy-versus-guy)].

**Why it matters.** The useful number is the gap between score and cost. At the top of the market, extra capability now costs more than proportionally more. Fable 5.2 at maximum effort scores about 10% higher than Opus 5.5 and costs about 2.6 times as much per task. Carbon scores within five points of it at about a fifth of the cost.

For a business buying AI, the choice is therefore a routing decision (sending each task to the cheapest model that can do it). The premium model is reserved for the work cheaper models fail.

Anthropic's own guidance points the same way: it tells most users to start with Opus 5.5. The reviews explain why. Ethan Mollick, a Wharton professor who writes One Useful Thing on applied AI, called it "'almost' AGI, or what I have called Jagged AGI" (artificial general intelligence, AI that matches people across most cognitive work). He found it strong across very different tasks but unable to judge its own output: a course that was correct but joyless, a paper that ran far too long, "and the AI did not notice these problems itself" [[One Useful Thing](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/fable-52-and-jagged-agi)]. Every, a media company that tests AI tools hands-on, reached the same split from the other direction. Its review called Fable 5.2 "the first model we've tested that lets professionals build production-grade tools of their own," and contrasted it with Opus 5.5, where "you're still in the loop, checking its work as you go" [[Every](https://every.to/vibe-check/fable-5-2)].

Read together, the reviews describe a model that finishes long projects but needs a person to judge whether the result is any good. That moves the human role from doing work to setting goals and judging results. For an operator, the opening is the projects that were never worth staffing: an internal tool, a data migration, a custom piece of software a small business could not have afforded to commission. The constraint is a person who can tell when the output is good.

**My take.** The ranking is signal: three independent evaluators (Artificial Analysis, ARC Prize and Bug Hunt Bench) put Fable 5.2 first. The labor-automation headline is not yet signal, because the 41.3% is the lab's own. The more important story is cost. A model at the top that most buyers should not use by default is the strongest evidence yet that the market is splitting into a premium tier and a value tier, and that the value tier is where most spending will go.

### 4. Google released Gemini 4 Carbon to everyone at a fifth of Fable's cost per task, and used it to prove parts of Signal's encryption correct

**What happened.** On November 10, Google made Gemini 4 Carbon generally available through its developer platform, its business plans and its top consumer tier [[Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-4-carbon-now-available/)]. Carbon was previewed in late October as the top model on Artificial Analysis's index but held to a small group of cyber defenders and partners. Google prices it at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens until November 30, then $4 and $20.

Google calls it its "strongest model with frontier performance in cybersecurity" and long agentic workflows (multi-step jobs a model carries out with tools, unsupervised). It still recommends its cheaper Gemini 4 Argon for most software and knowledge work. Carbon reads up to one million tokens at once, roughly 750,000 words, according to its model card [[Google DeepMind model card](https://deepmind.google/models/model-cards/gemini-4-carbon/)].

Independent results put it close to the top at much lower cost. Artificial Analysis scores Carbon 60 at high effort for $5.48 per task, five points behind Fable 5.2's maximum and one ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 [[Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/leaderboards/models)]. That cost appears to reflect the introductory price. Secondary coverage cites $7.76, so the figure should be expected to rise after November 30. On Bug Hunt Bench, Carbon fixed 57.3 of 105 planted bugs for $31.32, against 58 for $139.65 for Fable 5.2 [[Bug Hunt Bench data](https://bughunt.productcompass.pm/data/benchmark.json)].

The same day, Google DeepMind published a formal verification (a mathematical proof that software behaves as specified, rather than a test on examples) of the core of Signal, the encrypted messaging protocol also used inside WhatsApp [[Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/blog/formal-verification-signal-protocol)]. A new agent system, AlphaProof Code, running on Carbon, wrote more than six million lines of Lean (a language in which every step of a proof is checked by machine) over about four weeks.

It proved security properties of the compiled code for Signal's session setup and its message-encryption scheme. One property is that messages stay protected both before and after a device is temporarily compromised. Google calls it "the first time AI has been used to formally verify a widely deployed cryptographic protocol at this depth."

The limits are stated. Humans reviewed every specification (the precise statement of what was to be proved), and the agents flagged about 1% of the code for a second look. As a check, the team re-inserted two bugs that had previously been fixed, and the system proved both versions insecure. The proofs are open-source. The work covers the core cryptography, not the whole app, and assumes the underlying math is unbreakable and that no physical side channel leaks information [[AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/deepminds-alphaproof-code-verifies-signal-protocol-in-four-weeks)].

**Does Google's safety-testing framing hold up?** The AI Daily Brief reported that Google held Carbon back for voluntary safety testing by the US government (a review the company requests rather than one the law requires) [[AI Daily Brief, Nov 11](https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-11)]. Neither Google's launch post nor the model card says so. They name private security-testing firms hired to attack the model before release (Gray Swan, Trajectory Labs and Wiz), and no government body. Until Google or the government confirms it, the delay is unexplained rather than a precedent for government pre-release review.

**Why it matters.** Carbon resets the price of near-frontier work. If a model five points behind the leader costs a fifth as much, most buyers will use it for most tasks and keep the top model for the few that justify the bill (item 3). That pressure lands on Anthropic's pricing in the week its IPO is priced (item 2). Anthropic's revenue growth assumes customers keep paying premium rates for its best models.

The verification result matters for a different reason. Formal proofs have been too slow and expensive for anything but small, critical pieces of code. If AI agents can produce them in weeks, the cost of proving software correct falls sharply. That changes what buyers of critical software (banks, defense, medical devices) can demand from vendors.

It also changes the argument about AI and cybersecurity. Models that find flaws help attackers and defenders alike. A model that proves code has no flaw of a given kind helps only the defender.

**My take.** Carbon is the more consequential release of the week for anyone paying the bill, and Fable 5.2 the more consequential for what AI can do at its best. The Signal work is real but bounded: human-written specifications, the core protocol only, and a showcase chosen by Google. It is also the most concrete example yet of AI used to raise the security of existing software rather than to find holes in it.

### Dwarkesh Patel: the speed at which AI conquered mathematics is the best preview of how fast it will automate AI research, and he now expects automated AI researchers around 2028

*Dwarkesh Patel hosts the Dwarkesh Podcast, a long-form interview show with frontier-lab researchers and leaders, and writes essays on AI's trajectory. This entry is from his November 13 essay, "The avalanche that hit math is coming for AI development."*

Patel's claim is that he changed his mind this year, and he says why. Early in 2026 he would have put AI matching the best human AI researchers at least most of a decade away. He now puts his median at 2028, with a chance it comes as early as 2027, and AI better than the best human experts in nearly every field by about 2030 [[Dwarkesh Patel](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/rsi)]. His sharpest line: "I've now come to the view that we're only a couple of years away, and possibly only a couple of months away."

His argument runs through mathematics in steps. Models went from failing to add four-digit numbers in 2023, to gold-medal performance at the International Mathematical Olympiad in 2025, to solving open problems, to a Millennium Prize problem (one of seven famous unsolved problems carrying a $1 million prize) in about 88 hours. Critics answer that AI does well only on the checkable parts of math and cannot pose deep new questions. Patel accepts the observation and turns it around: "Current AI systems have solved a Millennium Prize problem, all without the ability to ask powerful questions."

He then argues AI research looks like that kind of math. Most progress comes from combining known ideas through many small experiments, each with a measurable result. He gives the transformer, the architecture behind today's models, as an example: its parts were already known, and combining them took persistence. Training loss (how far a model's predictions miss during training), efficiency of learning and speed of running a model all give numbers an AI can try to push up, so the work is trainable. In his words, "AI research is closer to formalizing a theorem than to writing a novel."

His evidence that this is already happening comes in three numbers. METR found Anthropic's progress on well-scoped tasks running about twice last year's pace, mostly attributed to AI-assisted research. OpenAI's heaviest researcher users of coding agents rose from about $1,000 to over $7,000 a day in spending between early summer and late August. And Anthropic reported Claude leading more than 26% of its AI research work as of August, from under 1% in February.

He gives two outcomes even odds. In one, AI improves faster than new ideas get harder to find, and progress compounds without new hardware. In the other, a burst delivers three to six years of progress in one year, then slows until more computing power arrives. He still gives about a 25% chance that automating top researchers slips past 2032.

The essay arrives the week Anthropic's IPO terms put a price on that expectation (item 2), and the week Mowshowitz argued Anthropic's own tests are falling behind its models (item 3). It extends the case Anthropic made in its November 2 filing, that its models "may imminently meet" its threshold for AI-driven acceleration. Patel builds the case from public evidence rather than from one company's internal estimates.

### ChinaTalk: American data vendors are selling Chinese labs the training environments that turn a capable model into a finished one, and export controls do not cover them

*Aqib Zakaria and Quinn Ennis, writing in ChinaTalk, Jordan Schneider's newsletter and podcast on US-China technology competition and chips. The article does not state the authors' backgrounds.*

The piece is about reinforcement learning environments (reinforcement learning trains a model by rewarding attempts that score well), the stage of AI training export controls have not reached. An RL environment is a simulated workspace where a model practises a task and is scored, then adjusted toward whatever scored well. The authors' example is a clone of PowerPoint paired with a grading rubric written by hired experts, which teaches a model to make well-designed slides [[ChinaTalk](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/the-us-is-selling-rl-environments)]. Their argument is that this stage sets a model's final capability: "RL is what gets the model to its final capability, whereas SFT is the intermediate step" (SFT, supervised fine-tuning, trains a model on worked examples before the reinforcement stage).

They name nine American vendors they say sell to Chinese labs. These include Mercor (to Alibaba), Surge AI (reportedly to Tencent and to the Chinese AI labs Zhipu and Moonshot), Turing (to Tencent and Alibaba) and several firms selling to ByteDance. The authors put the total at about half a billion dollars of such data sold to Chinese labs, a figure taken from earlier press reporting rather than their own count.

The mechanism they describe has three steps. First, vendors carry lessons from their American customers into products, so Chinese labs buy environments already proven to work and skip the trial and error. Second, the step where a model attempts tasks and is scored can take over 70% of this stage's training time, per a study they cite, and it is the step that leans hardest on fast memory, which Chinese chips lack. Better environments therefore save Chinese labs the resource they are shortest of. Third, coding environments can teach exploit-finding, and biology environments can teach gene synthesis, both dual-use.

They recommend that the Commerce Department treat dual-use environments under existing export rules, and that defense procurement rules bar contracted vendors from selling to Chinese labs of concern. They also note limits on the threat: Chinese buyers lag by several months, most environments are narrow, and labs increasingly build their own.

This is the export-control gap item 1's policy fight has not reached. Washington is closing the route for renting chips abroad. This piece argues the training know-how flows through a separate, unregulated channel, and it names one of the week's funding winners, Mercor (Worth a skim), among the sellers.

### Noahpinion: Noah Smith argues frontier AI firms will become conglomerates, because companies in every industry will need a tie-up with one to compete

*Noah Smith is an economist and former Bloomberg Opinion columnist who writes Noahpinion on economics and technology. He spoke with Brad DeLong, a University of California, Berkeley economist and former deputy assistant Treasury secretary. Dan Wang, a technology analyst and author of a book on China's engineering-led economy, joined them. This entry is from their November 9 discussion of the Anthropic IPO.*

Smith's claim concerns how the economy's ownership is structured, not whether AI replaces workers. He says he expects neither Anthropic ruling every industry nor wholesale job loss. Instead: "I'm envisioning a change in the *capital structure* of the efficiently producing economy" [[Noahpinion](https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/noah-brad-delong-and-dan-wang-discuss)].

His chain runs in four steps. A frontier lab's newest model gives an edge for several months before cheaper copies arrive. A firm that wants that edge needs privileged access, not a public subscription. Privileged access comes through a tie-up, such as an investment or a joint venture, that makes the firm in effect a subsidiary of the lab. Repeat this across industries and the result is a few AI conglomerates, each competing through a portfolio of affiliated companies: "Anthropic is simply the biggest of those fish in a oligopolistic ecosystem."

He grounds this in a 2026 paper by Imas, Koh and Legg that he describes as coming from Google DeepMind. The paper treats AI expertise as copyable and human expertise as fixed in supply. It finds that groups of complementary people can still beat AI on some tasks, but that AI coordinating its own work could remove the limit on firm size set by coordination costs (the cost of managing many people, which historically stops firms from growing without bound). If that limit falls, markets drift toward a few very large firms.

The transcript records that DeLong and Wang are more skeptical than Smith but does not give their specific objections. Smith discloses he holds no Anthropic stock, will probably buy at the IPO, and owns stock in TypeSafe, an Anthropic competitor.

The supplier placement in item 2 is a small version of the pattern he describes, run in reverse: chip, memory and cloud suppliers taking equity in the lab they depend on. Smith's view prices Anthropic's IPO as a stake in a future holding company, not a software vendor, which is a more generous frame than the trailing-revenue multiples in item 2's debate.

### Latent Space: the market for expert training data is moving from labelled answers to graded process, and Handshake built a billion-dollar business on it in a year

*Swyx (Shawn Wang) co-hosts Latent Space, the podcast and newsletter for AI engineers. He interviewed Garrett Lord, CEO of Handshake, and members of his team. Handshake runs a college careers network and now also sells expert-built training data and test environments to frontier labs.*

Handshake is the case study. Its AI training business sells labs specialised data, simulated work environments and benchmarks built on them, and the episode says it grew to about $1 billion of gross annualized revenue in roughly a year [[Latent Space](https://latent.space/p/handshake/)]. Its examples include a simulated bank with inboxes, documents and financial models for finance tasks, and a test of reading scientific images such as tissue slides.

The argument worth taking from the episode is that labs now pay for judgments about how a model worked, not just whether its answer was right. In the safety simulations, graders check which files an agent opened, whether it checked copies and what it did to its command history. The page notes that "A transcript of the agent's final reply would miss most of what" the graders are evaluating. In the finance example, a model could explain a tax rule when asked directly, but failed to see that one line of a spreadsheet model was where the rule applied. Knowing a rule and applying it at the right point are separate skills, and only an expert grader watching the process catches the gap.

Mercor's raise (Worth a skim) and ChinaTalk's report on environment sales to Chinese labs (above) are two sides of the same market. Together they describe one market from two angles: experts' judgment turned into scored practice that models learn from, sold to American labs and, per ChinaTalk, to Chinese ones too.

### In Good Company: Norway's sovereign wealth fund chief says companies are under-investing in AI adoption, and private equity is buying them to do it instead

*Nicolai Tangen is CEO of Norges Bank Investment Management, which runs Norway's roughly $2 trillion sovereign wealth fund and owns a slice of most listed companies worldwide.
